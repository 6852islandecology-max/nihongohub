# Chapter 3 — Municipal Office Procedures

> 📋 **What this chapter covers**: The four most important things to do at your local city/ward office within your first 14 days in Japan—plus the cultural cues that make those visits go smoothly. This chapter is general educational material. For your specific case, consult Appendix C's referral list.

---

## 3.1 The 14-day rule, and why it matters

Every long-term resident of Japan—anyone staying more than 90 days—is required to register their address at the local municipal office (市区町村役場, *shi-ku-chōson yakuba*) within **14 days of moving in**. This is not a polite request. It's a legal requirement under Japan's Basic Resident Registration Act (住民基本台帳法).

Why does this 14-day rule matter so much?

Because almost every other administrative interaction you'll have in Japan downstream depends on your **resident certificate** (住民票, *jūminhyō*). You can't open a bank account, sign a rental contract that requires identity verification, get a regular SIM card, or apply for a credit card without one.

In other words: the order of operations is locked. **Register your address first, then everything else becomes possible.**

There are some practical consequences of this:

- If you arrive in Japan on a Saturday but the offices are closed until Monday, the 14-day clock still starts ticking the day you moved in.
- If you change addresses later (a common scenario when you move from temporary housing to a long-term apartment), you re-register within 14 days at the new municipal office, and the old one removes you from their roll.
- Failure to register can theoretically result in a fine of up to ¥50,000, though in practice penalties are rare for genuine first-time mistakes.

The four core procedures most new residents need to complete in those first two weeks are:

1. **Resident registration** (住民票, *jūminhyō*) — mandatory
2. **My Number Card** (マイナンバーカード) — strongly recommended, technically optional
3. **National Health Insurance** (国民健康保険, *kokumin kenkō hoken*) — mandatory if not on workplace insurance
4. **National Pension** (国民年金, *kokumin nenkin*) — mandatory if you're 20–59 and not on workplace pension

We'll walk through each one.

## 3.2 Resident registration (住民票)

### What you'll bring

| Document | Where to get it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Residence Card (在留カード, *zairyū kādo*) | Issued at airport on arrival | The plastic card itself |
| Passport | — | Bring it even if your photo is on the residence card |
| Lease agreement or housing certificate | Your landlord, real-estate agent, or company | The agreement showing your new Japanese address |
| Personal seal (印鑑, *inkan*) | Optional at this stage | Some offices accept signatures, others want a stamp |

If you're moving in with someone who's already registered (a family member or a roommate already on the *jūminhyō*), bring proof of relationship—marriage certificate, birth certificate, or an official letter from the existing resident.

### What happens at the desk

You'll fill out a single form (転入届, *tennyū-todoke* — "moving-in notification"). The form is in Japanese, but at any office in a city with a sizeable foreign population, the staff will hand you a multilingual version (typically English, Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese, sometimes Spanish or Tagalog).

The form asks for:
- Your full name as it appears on your residence card
- Date of birth
- Sex
- New address in Japan
- Previous address (overseas)
- Date you moved in
- Family members who live with you (if any)
- Your residence status and period

When you hand it in, the staff takes your residence card to the back, prints your new address on the back of the card, and stamps the registration into their system. The whole interaction takes 30–60 minutes if there's no queue, longer at peak times (Monday mornings, end of March).

### What you walk out with

- **Residence card with your new address printed on the back.** This is now your primary ID for almost everything.
- **A *jūminhyō* certificate** (住民票記載事項証明書, *jūminhyō kisai jikō shōmeisho*), if you ask for one—usually ¥300 per copy. Get at least two; you'll use them within the next month for bank accounts and SIM contracts.

### Common surprises

- **Your address is now fixed in their system.** If you misspelled the building name or the room number, fixing it later requires a separate procedure. Double-check before you walk out.
- **The certificate has a date attached.** Most banks and SIM providers will only accept a *jūminhyō* issued within the last 3 months, so don't print 10 copies on day 1; print them as you need them.
- **Bring cash.** Many municipal offices still don't accept credit cards for the certificate fee. ¥1,000 in coins covers a few copies.

## 3.3 My Number Card (マイナンバーカード)

### What it is, and what it isn't

The My Number system, launched in 2016, assigns every resident of Japan—Japanese citizens and foreign residents alike—a 12-digit personal identification number called the **Individual Number** (個人番号, *kojin bangō*) or, in everyday speech, the *My Number*.

After you register your address, the municipal office automatically mails you a **Notification Card** (通知カード, *tsūchi kādo*)—a paper card showing your number—within 2–4 weeks. This paper card is technically enough to use the My Number system: you write the number on tax forms, employment paperwork, and so on.

But you can also apply for the **My Number Card** (the plastic ID card), which is technically optional. Why bother?

Because the plastic card is, in practical terms, the closest thing modern Japan has to a universal ID:

- It links to your **Health Insurance card** (mandatory linkage as of December 2024—you can still use the old paper card until the transition completes, but new issuances are paused for non-card holders)
- It enables **e-Tax** for online tax filing (saves you a trip to the tax office)
- It's accepted as **photo ID** at banks, government counters, and increasingly at private services
- It allows you to print **certificates** (residence certificate, tax certificate, family register) at convenience stores 24/7 instead of waiting at the municipal office

### How to apply

There are three application paths:

**Path A: Online via smartphone (most common)**

1. Receive your Notification Card by mail (2–4 weeks after registration)
2. Use the QR code on the card to access the application portal
3. Take a passport-style photo of yourself with your phone
4. Submit; wait 1–2 months for the actual card
5. Visit the municipal office to pick it up (this visit is in person—they verify your identity)

**Path B: Paper application**

1. Use the paper application form mailed with the Notification Card
2. Attach a photo, mail it to the listed address
3. Same 1–2 month wait, same in-person pickup

**Path C: At the municipal office**

Some larger municipal offices have on-site application booths where you can take the photo and submit on the spot. You still have to come back to pick up the card 1–2 months later.

### Common surprises

- **The PIN.** When you pick up the card, you set up to four different PINs (4-digit signing PIN, 6–16 character authentication PIN, 4-digit user-certificate PIN, 4-digit JPKI PIN). Write them down somewhere safe. Forgetting them means going back to the office to reset.
- **The card has an expiration.** For foreign residents, the card expires when your residence period expires. If you renew your visa, you need to update the card.
- **Don't laminate it or punch holes in it.** It contains an IC chip; physical damage voids it.

## 3.4 National Health Insurance (国民健康保険)

### Who needs to enroll, and who doesn't

If you're employed by a Japanese company with social insurance (社会保険, *shakai hoken*), your employer enrolls you and deducts the premiums from your paycheck. You **don't need to enroll** in National Health Insurance separately. Your insurance card will arrive at the office within 2–3 weeks of starting work.

If you're **self-employed, a student, on a working holiday, or unemployed**, you enroll in National Health Insurance at the municipal office, ideally on the same day you register your address.

### What you'll bring

- Residence Card
- *Jūminhyō* (or proof of registration that day)
- Personal seal (some offices) or signature
- Any previous insurance documentation if you had Japanese coverage before (most new residents won't)

### What you'll pay

The premium is calculated based on:
- Your previous year's income (in Japan—if you have none, it starts at the minimum)
- The number of family members on the insurance
- Your municipality's local rate (it varies)

For a new resident with no Japanese income history, expect to pay roughly **¥1,500–¥3,000 per month** in the first year. Once you file Japanese taxes (the year after starting work), the premium adjusts to reflect your actual income—often increasing significantly.

The premium covers about **70% of medical costs** (you pay 30% out of pocket at the clinic or hospital). For high-cost treatment, there's an additional safety net called the **High-Cost Medical Care Benefit** (高額療養費制度, *kōgaku ryōyōhi seido*) that caps your monthly out-of-pocket at roughly ¥80,000 for typical income levels.

### Common surprises

- **Back-payment.** If you registered your address late (say, Day 30 instead of Day 14), you'll be charged premiums retroactive to the day you moved in. This catches a lot of people off guard.
- **The card arrives separately.** Don't expect to walk out with a physical insurance card. It typically arrives in the mail 1–2 weeks later.
- **Voluntary withdrawal isn't allowed.** Once you're enrolled, you can't simply "cancel." You exit only by leaving Japan, joining workplace insurance, or qualifying for an exemption.

## 3.5 National Pension (国民年金)

### Who needs to enroll

Anyone aged **20 to 59** who is registered as a resident of Japan, regardless of nationality, is required to enroll in the National Pension System (国民年金). Like National Health Insurance, if you're on workplace social insurance, your employer handles it (it becomes 厚生年金, *kōsei nenkin*, "Welfare Pension"—a separate but linked tier).

Self-employed, students, working-holiday makers, and unemployed residents enroll in National Pension at the same municipal office desk that handles National Health Insurance.

### What you'll pay

The flat-rate premium is **¥17,510 per month** (FY2026 figure—it adjusts annually). Yes, that's a lot—roughly ¥210,000 a year.

For students, there's a **Student Payment Exemption System** (学生納付特例制度) that lets you defer payments while enrolled in a recognized educational institution. You apply at the municipal office with your student ID and an enrollment certificate.

For low-income working residents, there's a partial exemption system based on income tiers. The municipal staff will explain which tier applies to you.

### What happens when you leave Japan

If you leave Japan permanently after contributing to the National Pension for at least 6 months (and less than 25 years), you may be eligible for a **Lump-sum Withdrawal Payment** (脱退一時金, *dattai ichijikin*). The application is filed within 2 years of leaving Japan. The amount depends on how many months you contributed, capped at 60 months.

This is a frequently misunderstood point: many short-term residents assume their pension contributions are "lost." They're not, but you have to apply for the refund—it's not automatic.

### Common surprises

- **The Pension Notebook (年金手帳, *nenkin techō*) has been phased out.** New enrollees as of 2022 onward receive a Basic Pension Number Notification Document instead. Keep it; you'll need the number throughout your career.
- **Late payments accrue.** If you ignore the bills, premiums keep accumulating. Don't ignore them—if you can't afford the payments, apply for the exemption proactively.
- **The system is portable across municipalities.** When you move within Japan, your pension record follows you automatically.

## 3.6 A worked example: Yamada Tarō's first two weeks

To bring all of this together, here's a fictional walkthrough using a generic name, **Yamada Tarō** (山田 太郎).

> *Yamada Tarō is a fictional character. The names, dates, and addresses below are illustrative only. Your specific situation may differ.*

| Day | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | May 1 | Tarō arrives at Narita Airport on a Student Visa. Receives his Residence Card at immigration. Takes the train to his pre-arranged share-house in Shinjuku, Tokyo. |
| 2 | May 2 | Picks up the share-house's lease agreement copy from the manager. Buys ¥2,000 in 100-yen coins for office fees. |
| 3 | May 3 (Holiday) | Constitution Day, all offices closed. Goes sightseeing. |
| 4 | May 4 (Holiday) | Greenery Day, offices closed. |
| 5 | May 5 (Holiday) | Children's Day, offices closed. |
| 6 | May 6 | First Tuesday post-Golden Week. Visits Shinjuku Ward Office at 09:30 (queue hits its longest at 10:30). Brings: Residence Card, passport, lease agreement, ¥2,000 cash. |
| | | At Window 12 (Resident Registration), fills out the multilingual *tennyū-todoke* form. Address gets stamped on the back of his Residence Card. Takes 45 minutes. |
| | | Walks to Window 18 (National Health Insurance + National Pension). Enrolls in NHI (premium estimated at ¥1,800/month for first year). Applies for the Student Payment Exemption for the National Pension—approved on the spot because he brought his student certificate from his Japanese language school. |
| | | Picks up two copies of his *jūminhyō* certificate at Window 5 (¥300 each). |
| | | Total time at the office: 2 hours 15 minutes. Walks out with all four procedures complete. |
| 9 | May 9 | The paper Notification Card with his My Number arrives in the mail. Files it carefully; he'll apply for the plastic My Number Card the following week. |
| 10 | May 10 | Goes to Mizuho Bank with his Residence Card (now showing the new address) and a *jūminhyō* certificate. Opens a basic deposit account. |
| 11 | May 11 | Subscribes to a Mineo MVNO SIM plan online using his new bank account. SIM arrives in 3 business days. |
| 13 | May 13 | NHI insurance card arrives in the mail. He stores it in his wallet. |
| 18 | May 18 | Submits the My Number Card application via QR code on the Notification Card. |

In real life, the pace varies—Golden Week is an extreme example of the kind of dead zone that can compress your usable window. But the order of operations holds: address → My Number → bank → SIM → My Number Card pickup later.

## 3.7 The cultural texture: how to behave at the municipal office

A few small things go a long way at a Japanese municipal office.

**Dress quietly.** Casual clean is fine—jeans, a shirt, no rips. But consider that the staff dress in muted colors and the entire space is built for low-key bureaucratic interaction. A loud hoodie or cropped tank reads as out-of-place; you'll be served either way, but the rapport tilts in your favor when you fit the room's tone.

**Use full names.** When the staff calls "山田 太郎 様" (*Yamada Tarō-sama*) over the loudspeaker, that's you. The 様 (*sama*) suffix is reflexive; you don't address yourself with it. When you reach the counter, just say "はい" (*hai*, "yes") or "山田です" (*Yamada desu*, "I'm Yamada").

**Bring more documents than you need.** Bureaucrats prefer redundancy. Even if the website lists three required documents, bring the originals plus copies of each, your residence card, your passport, and any previous correspondence. Worst case, the extras stay in your bag.

**Slow down.** Japanese bureaucratic procedures are designed around methodical verification, not speed. Trying to rush—asking impatient follow-up questions, glancing at your phone—reads as disrespectful. Settle into the pace; the queue moves when it moves.

**Pay attention to small "okayness" cues.** "大丈夫です" (*daijōbu desu*) literally means "it's okay" but carries different weight depending on tone: sometimes it's "no problem, proceed," sometimes it's "actually I'm declining your offer politely." Listen for it.
