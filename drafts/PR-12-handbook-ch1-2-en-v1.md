# Chapter 1 — Before You Arrive

## 1.1 The five things to settle before getting on the plane

Most of the trouble new residents of Japan run into in their first month is rooted in things that could have been settled before departure. Five items in particular pay disproportionate dividends:

1. **Understand which residence status you actually hold.** "I have a Japanese visa" is not a useful sentence; "I have a Student (留学) visa for 2 years 3 months, with no work permission yet" is. You'll repeat this sentence dozens of times in the first month.
2. **Have a fixed address you can prove on paper.** Many of the procedures in Chapter 3 depend on it. Even temporary housing for the first 30 days is fine, as long as you have a written agreement.
3. **Understand which kind of bank account you'll qualify for.** Major banks won't open a regular account for a resident with less than 6 months of confirmed residence, in some cases. Workarounds exist (Yucho, Shinsei, online-first banks), but knowing this in advance saves a frustrating week.
4. **Know whether you need an *inkan* (personal seal) or a signature.** As of 2026, signatures are accepted for most official procedures, but inkan are still required at some banks, real-estate offices, and traditional employers. If you'll be opening a bank account that requires one, you need one before you can open the account.
5. **Decide what to do about a SIM card on Day 1.** A short-term tourist SIM at the airport keeps you connected during the 30 days it takes to get a long-term plan via your new bank account. Trying to do everything at once on Day 1 is the most common cause of locked-out moments.

## 1.2 Residence status, in plain language

Japan has roughly 30 categories of residence status, but for most new residents, four matter:

| Category | Typical holder | Period | Work permission |
|---|---|---|---|
| 留学 (Student) | University student, language school student | 1 year 3 months → 4 years 3 months | Up to 28 hours/week with separate permit |
| 技術・人文知識・国際業務 (Engineer/Humanities/International Services) | Most white-collar foreign workers | 1, 3, or 5 years | Yes, within the role described in the visa application |
| 特定技能 (Specified Skilled Worker) | Manufacturing, hospitality, agriculture, care | 1, 5 years | Yes, within the designated industry |
| 配偶者ビザ (Spouse Visa) | Spouse of a Japanese national or permanent resident | 1, 3, 5 years | Yes, no restrictions |

There are many others—Working Holiday, Designated Activities (specifying internships, etc.), Permanent Resident, and so on. Your residence card lists yours under "在留資格" (*zairyū shikaku*).

If you don't yet know which category yours falls under, three places give you authoritative information without paying anyone:

- The Immigration Services Agency portal (https://www.moj.go.jp/isa/) — the source-of-truth page in English
- The application paperwork your employer or school filed on your behalf
- The Certificate of Eligibility (在留資格認定証明書) you received before applying for your visa stamp

We're not going to walk through how to choose your status, because that decision is made before this handbook is useful—it happens during the visa application phase, often through your employer's HR or your school's international office. If your situation is unusual (changing categories, applying without a sponsor, a denied application), Appendix C lists referral resources.

## 1.3 The address paradox

One of the strangest things about moving to Japan is that the housing market and the bureaucratic system are slightly out of sync. To rent an apartment, you need a residence card and (often) a Japanese guarantor. To get the residence card showing your new address, you need to register at the municipal office. To register, you need a lease agreement with that address.

The way this is solved in practice:

- **Short-term housing first.** Most new arrivals book 30 days at a share house, an Airbnb-style monthly rental (called *monthly mansion* in Japan), or a corporate-housing arrangement set up by an employer. This counts as a valid address for registration.
- **Then long-term housing search.** With your registered address and a *jūminhyō* certificate in hand, you have the documentation needed for a regular long-term lease (賃貸契約).
- **Re-register when you move.** When you move to the long-term apartment, you do the address-change procedure (転居届) at the same municipal office. It takes about 30 minutes.

This two-step process is so common that the entire industry of "monthly mansion" rentals has organized itself around it. If you're searching for short-term housing online, terms like *minpaku* (民泊), *monthly mansion*, *share house*, and *gaijin house* will turn up the relevant listings. None of these need a guarantor.

## 1.4 Bank accounts, before and after

Japan has roughly four "tiers" of banks for new residents:

**Tier 1 — Universal-friendly accounts (open these first if needed)**

- **Yucho Bank (ゆうちょ銀行).** The post office bank. Accepts all residents regardless of how long they've been in Japan. Slightly limited features (no IC chip cards, no overseas wires from some branches), but it gets you a working account in Japan on Week 1 if you must.
- **Shinsei Bank (新生銀行) / SBI.** Online-first, English support, no Japanese guarantor required. Their PowerFlex account is popular with foreign residents because the application is mostly online and processes within 1 week.

**Tier 2 — Mainstream banks (typical 6-month residence requirement)**

- **Mitsubishi UFJ (三菱UFJ), Sumitomo Mitsui (SMBC), Mizuho (みずほ).** These are the "city banks." They generally require you to have lived in Japan for 6 months before opening a regular account, but accept residents with workplace introduction sooner. Walk into a branch with your *jūminhyō*, residence card, and an *inkan* (some require this).

**Tier 3 — Local credit unions**

- **Shinkin (信用金庫), regional banks.** Often more flexible than the city banks for newcomers, especially if you have a local employer or a Japanese friend who can introduce you. Worth asking about.

**Tier 4 — Online challenger banks**

- **Sony Bank, Rakuten Bank, JNB.** Generally require an existing Japanese bank account or proof of stable residence. Not the right starting point for a new resident, but useful as a second account once you're established.

Don't open more accounts than you need in the first month. Two is usually enough: one for receiving income (workplace requires their account or accepts whatever you have), one as backup. Adding more later is painless.

## 1.5 The inkan question

A personal seal—an *inkan* or *hanko* (印鑑 / 判子)—is a small carved cylinder, usually wooden or plastic, with your family name in characters. Stamping it (with red oil-based ink, *shuniku*) is the Japanese equivalent of signing.

There are three kinds:

- **Mitome-in (認印).** Informal, for everyday acknowledgments (package deliveries, internal company forms). Costs around ¥500–¥2,000 at a 100-yen shop or stationery store.
- **Ginkō-in (銀行印).** Registered with your bank. Required by some banks for account opening; others accept signatures.
- **Jitsu-in (実印).** Officially registered at your municipal office; used for major legal contracts (real estate purchase, marriage). Most new residents won't need one in their first year.

For a new resident, here's the practical sequence:

1. Skip the inkan for the first week. Use signatures wherever possible.
2. If you arrive at a bank counter and they require an inkan to open the account, walk to the nearest 100-yen shop or stationery store. Pre-made inkan with common Japanese surnames are sold at 100-yen shops. For non-Japanese names (e.g., Smith, García, Rahman, 王), specialized shops can carve a custom one for ¥3,000–¥10,000 in 1–2 days.
3. Decide later whether you need the more formal *jitsu-in*. Most new residents don't, until they're buying property or signing a marriage paperwork.

## 1.6 SIM cards and the Day-1 communication problem

When you land in Japan, you have three main options for getting connected on Day 1:

| Option | Cost (first month) | Setup | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tourist SIM at the airport | ¥3,000–¥5,000 | Buy at the kiosk on arrival | Plastic SIM, no Japanese ID needed; valid 30 days |
| eSIM (digital, pre-purchased) | ¥1,500–¥3,500 | Activate before flight | Saves the airport kiosk wait; same 30-day validity |
| Pocket Wi-Fi rental | ¥4,000–¥7,000 | Pickup at airport | Useful for a family/small group; you carry a small device |

Don't try to sign up for a long-term Japanese SIM contract on Day 1. You don't yet have your address registered, you don't have a Japanese bank account, and you'd be wasting two weeks of contract time anyway. The 30-day stopgap is the right play.

After your municipal-office visit (Chapter 3) and bank-account opening (Week 2), you'll switch to a long-term plan. Major options:

- **Major carriers (docomo, au, SoftBank).** Premium pricing (¥4,000–¥7,000/month for unlimited data plans), high-quality network, English support varies by location.
- **MVNOs (IIJmio, mineo, OCN, Y!mobile, Rakuten Mobile, etc.).** Run on the major carriers' networks, offer plans from ¥1,000–¥3,000/month for typical usage. English support is rare; the websites are usually only in Japanese, but Google Translate handles them well.

## 1.7 What to pack (administrative kit)

This isn't your suitcase list. It's the document folder that goes in your carry-on:

- Original passport
- Original Certificate of Eligibility (在留資格認定証明書)
- 2–3 passport-sized photographs (for any unexpected applications)
- Lease agreement or housing arrangement letter (printed)
- Letter from employer/school confirming the role/enrollment
- Bank statement showing financial means (some processes ask)
- Health insurance documentation from your home country (helpful for any pre-existing conditions or interim care)
- Emergency-contact information for someone who can authenticate your identity by phone if something goes wrong

Don't keep these only on your phone. Print physical copies. Bureaucratic processes in Japan still favor paper, and you don't want to be at a service counter watching your phone die.

# Chapter 2 — Your First Week in Japan

## 2.1 The first 7 days, in order

Once you arrive in Japan, the first seven days carry asymmetric leverage. What you set up well in this window saves weeks of friction later. What you let drift creates compounding problems.

The order that works for most arrivals:

| Day | Priority | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arrive, pick up Residence Card at airport, get to housing | Connected, sleeping somewhere with an address |
| 2 | Confirm housing paperwork, get a tourist SIM if you didn't get an eSIM | Communication functional |
| 3 | Buy basic supplies, scout the route to your municipal office | Ready for paperwork |
| 4–7 | Visit municipal office (Chapter 3), open a bank account, exchange first cash | Official resident with banking |
| 7+ | Switch from tourist SIM to a long-term plan | Stable monthly costs |

This is intentionally relaxed compared to the more aggressive "do everything on Day 2" advice you'll find on travel blogs. Frustration scales nonlinearly with sleep debt, and the reality is that most municipal offices are only open Monday to Friday, 09:00–17:00, with 12:00–13:00 lunch closures. There is no reward for cramming.

## 2.2 What happens at the airport

When you land, before you reach baggage claim, you'll be directed to the immigration counter. If your visa application included designation as a "specified resident" (中長期在留者), you'll receive your Residence Card on the spot. A small color-printed card, typically lavender for student status, blue for work statuses.

Things to verify before you walk away from the immigration counter:

- The name on the card matches your passport spelling exactly
- Your residence period is correct (look for "在留期間: 2 年" or similar)
- Your residence status is correct
- The expiry date on the card

If anything is wrong, raise it immediately. Correcting it later involves visiting an Immigration Services Agency office, which is an ordeal compared to a 30-second fix at the counter.

If you arrive at a smaller airport that doesn't issue Residence Cards on the spot (some regional airports), you'll have a Designation of Place of Residence in your passport with a notation that the card will be mailed to you within 14 days of registering your address. Treat this as your residence card for the interim.

## 2.3 Getting from the airport to your housing

Three Tokyo-area options give a sense of how this works:

- **Narita Airport → Tokyo.** Narita Express (60 minutes, ¥3,070), Keisei Skyliner (45 minutes, ¥2,580), or Limousine Bus (90 minutes, ¥3,200). Skyliner is usually fastest if you're heading to Ueno or northern Tokyo.
- **Haneda Airport → Tokyo.** Tokyo Monorail + JR Yamanote (45 minutes total, ¥650), Keikyu Line (25 minutes to Shinagawa, ¥320), Limousine Bus (60 minutes, ¥1,200). Closer airport, cheaper transit.
- **Kansai International → Osaka.** JR Haruka (50 minutes, ¥3,400), Nankai Rapi:t (40 minutes, ¥1,490), Limousine Bus (50 minutes, ¥1,950).

Rule of thumb: limousine buses are easier with luggage but more weather-dependent and traffic-dependent. Trains are reliable but require lifting your suitcase up stairs at older stations.

For your destination address, save the full Japanese-language version of the address before you fly. Show it to taxi drivers, station attendants, hotel front desks, and anyone helping you navigate. Latin-letter spellings of Japanese addresses confuse even Japanese-speaking GPS systems.

## 2.4 The first SIM card, in 10 minutes at the airport

If you didn't pre-purchase an eSIM, walk to the SIM kiosk in the international arrivals lobby. Major options:

| Provider | Plan | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| docomo Visitor SIM | 7 GB / 30 days | ¥3,500 | Premier network coverage |
| Sakura Mobile | 30-day plan | ¥3,800 | Has English staff and English customer support |
| Mobal | 30-day plan | ¥3,300 | English friendly, monthly rolling option |

Plug it in, set up the APN per the instructions, and you have data immediately. You won't have a Japanese phone number you can give out yet (this requires a long-term plan), but messaging apps (LINE, WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal) keep you reachable.

If you're traveling with family, consider a pocket Wi-Fi rental instead. One device, multiple users, similar price.

## 2.5 The first 24 hours: settling in

This is the unstructured part. Most new arrivals to Japan are jet-lagged and overwhelmed; the to-do list can wait a day. A practical 24-hour shape:

- **Hour 0–2.** Get to your housing, drop bags, drink water, eat something simple (a convenience-store onigiri is fine).
- **Hour 2–4.** Walk a 15-minute radius around your housing. Note the nearest convenience store, train station, supermarket, post office, drugstore. These five locations cover 80% of your daily needs.
- **Hour 4–8.** Sleep if you can. Local timezone adjustment is more important than maximizing the day.
- **Hour 8+.** Light errand: buy a SIM (if not already), grab a small spiral notebook to write addresses and names in. Take a photo of your residence card and back it up to cloud storage.

The thing to resist on Day 1 is the urge to "be productive." You'll function noticeably better after one full sleep cycle in the new timezone.

## 2.6 Setting up daily routines that hold

Three habits that compound over the first month:

- **Always carry the Residence Card.** Foreign residents are required by law to carry it at all times. Police checks are uncommon but happen; not having it is a fineable offense (¥200,000 max in theory, in practice usually a warning for first-time mistakes).
- **Keep ¥10,000 cash on you.** Even though Japan is increasingly card-friendly, some smaller restaurants, taxis at night, and older shops still take cash only. ¥10,000 comfortably covers a day's surprises.
- **Use your *jūminhyō* certificates strategically.** Once you have them (Chapter 3), you'll use them for bank accounts, SIM contracts, leases. Don't print them in advance—they expire after 3 months at most institutions.

## 2.7 Common surprises in week one

- **Convenience stores are administrative anchors.** You can pay utility bills, ship packages, print documents, withdraw cash from international ATMs (Seven Bank, Lawson Bank), buy stamps, and—after you have the My Number Card—print *jūminhyō* certificates 24/7. Lean into this.
- **The post office matters.** Yucho Bank ATMs are inside post offices and accept most foreign cards for cash withdrawal. Most foreign residents use Yucho or Seven Bank ATMs for the first month before getting a Japanese debit card.
- **Cards aren't everywhere.** Restaurants and small shops in residential neighborhoods often still don't take card. Carry cash.
- **Garbage takes 3 weeks to figure out.** It's the most consistently confusing part of moving to Japan. We cover the rules in Chapter 4.
