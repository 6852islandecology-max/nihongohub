Who this is for

There is a whole way of experiencing Japan that the guidebooks miss. Too long for a hotel, too short to move in — the three-to-six-month stay. The remote worker doing a season from Japan. The traveller on a sabbatical or a career break. The retiree who wants one long, slow autumn in Kyoto instead of a rushed week.

This playbook is for that stay. It assumes you have already sorted out how long you are legally allowed to be in Japan (see the note above) and focuses on the part nobody explains well: how to actually live here for months without the full machinery of relocation — no city-hall residence registration, no apartment lease guarantor, no local bank account required.

THE BIG IDEAA long stay is not a long holiday. The trick is to stop paying tourist prices for everything: book accommodation by the month, get a real data plan, pick one base and travel from it, and shop where locals shop. Do that and Japan becomes dramatically cheaper than a two-week trip — per day.

BOOK FOR THE STAY LENGTHMany vacation-rental and hotel platforms automatically apply a weekly or monthly discount once you enter 7+ or 28+ nights — sometimes 20–50% off the nightly rate. 3 · Staying connected

For a long stay you need reliable data from the minute you land — for maps, translation, bookings, and (if you work) video calls.

Data: eSIM vs pocket Wi-Fi

An eSIM activates on your phone before you even land — no physical SIM, no queue at the airport. It's the simplest option for one device. If you carry a laptop and a phone, or travel as a couple, a pocket Wi-Fi router shares one connection across several devices and is rented per day or month.

Working from Japan

If you're here to work remotely, two things matter: the time zone (Japan is UTC+9 — great for Asia-Pacific teams, brutal for US-morning calls) and a place to work. Coworking spaces in Tokyo, Osaka and Fukuoka offer day passes (¥1,000–2,500) and monthly memberships; many cafés tolerate laptops, but Japan has its own café etiquette — buy something, don't camp at peak times, keep calls quiet (or step outside).

FREE BACK-UPMajor train stations, convenience stores, and city tourist offices have free Wi-Fi. It's not enough to work on, but it's a lifesaver if your data runs out mid-trip.

4 · Money without becoming a resident

You don't need a Japanese bank account for a long stay. You need a plan for cash, cards, and the one transport card that runs your daily life.

  • Japan still loves cash. Many restaurants, small shops, shrines, and rural buses are cash-only. Carry more than you think — ¥10,000–20,000 on you is normal.
  • Where to withdraw: 7-Bank ATMs (inside every 7-Eleven) and Japan Post Bank ATMs reliably accept foreign cards, in English, 24/7. Most other bank ATMs reject foreign cards.
  • Cards: bring a no-foreign-fee debit/credit card and a back-up on a different network (Visa + Mastercard). Tell your bank you'll be in Japan so they don't freeze it.
  • Tap-to-pay is spreading but not universal — never rely on it as your only method.
  • Tipping doesn't exist. The price is the price. Don't leave money on the table; it causes confusion.

The card that runs your day: IC cards

A rechargeable IC card (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA and regional siblings) taps you onto almost every train and bus nationwide, and pays at convenience stores, vending machines, and many shops. You can add a Suica or PASMO to an iPhone's Apple Wallet and top it up with a foreign card — no physical card, no counter. For a months-long stay this is the single most useful thing to set up on day one.

CHECK CURRENT CARD RULESAvailability of physical welcome/tourist IC cards and the mobile-Suica top-up rules change periodically. Check the current situation when you arrive — but the mobile IC card in your phone is the most future-proof choice.

5 · Health & insurance for a long visit

As a short-term visitor you are not in Japan's national health insurance system, so you pay out of pocket — which means travel/medical insurance is not optional for a multi-month stay.

  • Get insurance that actually covers a long trip. Standard 2-week travel policies may cap at 30, 60, or 90 days — read the duration limit. Remote-worker / "nomad" insurers and long-stay travel policies are built for exactly this. Compare coverage for hospitalisation, medical evacuation, and (if relevant) any pre-existing conditions.
  • Pharmacies (薬局) handle minor ailments well; a pharmacist can recommend over-the-counter remedies. Bring enough of any prescription medication for your whole stay, in its original packaging, with a copy of the prescription — and check in advance that it's legal to bring into Japan (some common medicines are restricted).
  • Finding an English-speaking clinic: the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) maintains an online directory of medical institutions that serve visitors in English. Save the link before you travel.
  • Emergencies: 119 for ambulance/fire, 110 for police. Ambulances are free to call, but you pay for treatment.
A FEW PHRASES WORTH SAVING
  • 病院はどこですか。Byōin wa doko desu ka. — Where is the hospital?
  • 英語が話せる先生はいますか。Eigo ga hanaseru sensei wa imasu ka. — Is there an English-speaking doctor?
  • 薬がほしいです。Kusuri ga hoshii desu. — I'd like some medicine.

This is general information, not medical advice. Confirm coverage details with your insurer and bring any prescriptions you rely on.

6 · Getting around for months

Over a long stay the maths changes: a base city plus occasional long trips beats a rail pass you bought for a two-week dash.

  • Daily life: your IC card (Section 4) covers local trains and buses — just tap.
  • The nationwide JR Pass only pays off if you're moving long distances frequently in a short window. For a slow base-and-trips stay, point-to-point tickets or a regional pass (JR East, JR West, Kyushu, etc.) usually cost less. Do the maths for your actual route before buying.
  • Long distances on a budget: overnight buses and budget domestic flights (Peach, Jetstar Japan, Skymark) are far cheaper than the bullet train for the same trip if you book ahead.

7 · Living like a temporary local

The difference between a long holiday and a long stay is routine. Here's how to build a cheap, comfortable daily life fast.

Eat well for less

  • Supermarkets beat restaurants for a long stay, and most discount fresh food (お惣菜, bento) by 20–50% in the last hour before closing — look for the 半額 (half-price) stickers.
  • Convenience stores (conbini) are genuinely good and cheap for breakfast, coffee, and a quick meal — and they're everywhere.
  • Set lunches (定食 / ランチ) at local restaurants are a fraction of dinner prices for the same kitchen. Eat your big meal at noon.
  • Want to go deeper into the food culture — markets, izakaya etiquette, a cooking class? A guided food experience early in your stay teaches you where the locals actually eat.

The rest of daily life

  • Laundry: coin laundries (コインランドリー) are everywhere if your accommodation lacks a machine.
  • Gyms & pools: municipal sports centres offer cheap day passes; commercial gyms do monthly plans.
  • Libraries & parks are free, quiet, and a lovely part of a slow stay.
  • Meet people: language-exchange meetups and hobby circles are the fastest way to make a city feel like home.

Keep learning the language

A few months in Japan is the best chance you'll ever get to actually use Japanese. Even an hour a week of speaking practice turns "tourist phrases" into real conversations — and locals open up the moment you try.

LEAVING CLEANLY — END-OF-STAY CHECKLIST
  • Accommodation check-out date & any cleaning/key-return rules confirmed
  • Pocket Wi-Fi returned (envelope at the airport or post) — late returns get charged
  • IC card balance spent down (or kept — it doesn't expire)
  • Tax-free shopping receipts ready for the airport, if you bought duty-free
  • Cancelled any monthly memberships (gym, coworking)
