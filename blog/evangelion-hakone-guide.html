Evangelion in Hakone: visiting the real Tokyo-3

UPDATED 2026-07 · TIE-UPS & STORE HOURS CHANGE — CONFIRM LOCALLY BEFORE A DETOUR

In Neon Genesis Evangelion, humanity's fortress city Tokyo-3 rises from a caldera ringed by mountains and a lake. That caldera is real: it's Hakone, the hot-spring resort 90 minutes from Tokyo. The series maps onto the region with unusual precision — Lake Ashi, the ropeway, Sengokuhara's grasslands — and the town has embraced it with a NERV-styled "outpost", Eva-themed signboards and an official Evangelion store. This guide shows where the Evangelion layer of Hakone is, how it lines up with the fiction, and how to fold the pilgrimage into an ordinary onsen trip — plus the truth about the "Eva manholes" some guides promise, and the Japanese you'll use.

Why Hakone is Tokyo-3

Evangelion's creators set Tokyo-3 in the Hakone caldera explicitly — the show's maps, station names and terrain are drawn from the real geography of this corner of Kanagawa. Sengokuhara, the highland district in the caldera's north, is where much of the city's fictional footprint sits; Lake Ashi (Ashinoko) appears in key battles; and the Hakone Ropeway's Tōgendai Station is the model for the station where characters descend toward NERV headquarters.

What makes Hakone unusual among anime pilgrimage destinations is that the place reciprocates. A major "Evangelion × Hakone" tourism campaign in 2020 — run by the Odakyū Group and Fujita Kanko, the region's transport and hospitality companies — dressed stations and facilities in NERV styling, and pieces of the collaboration have stayed visible since: themed signboards, shop tie-ups and a town-built NERV "outpost" make the fiction legible on the street rather than something only fans can see. It's a cousin of the Gundam manhole towns pattern — a franchise and local partners drawing fans into a real place.

NERV on the street — and the Eva manhole myth

What you'll genuinely find at street level: Eva-styled guide boards and station decor left from the town and rail-group tie-ups, and the NERV outpost in Kintoki Park (below). Spotting NERV styling scattered through an otherwise ordinary onsen town is exactly the joke the collaboration intends.

One correction to what you may have read elsewhere: several online guides claim there are Evangelion manhole covers near Hakone-Yumoto Station. We could not verify any such covers — they don't appear in the town's official Evangelion pages, in on-the-ground pilgrimage reports, or in Japanese-language sources. If Eva drain covers are what you're after, the confirmed ones are Tokorozawa's solar-lit LED covers in Saitama (Rei and Asuka among them), which glow after dark — part of Japan's wider character manhole tradition. And note that character covers generally do not come with a collectible manhole card — check the official card list separately if you want the card too.

🧭 Pilgrimage etiquette, Hakone edition
  • The school site is private ground. The former Sengokuhara JHS (closed 2008) has been repurposed — view the exterior from public ground and don't enter.
  • Campaign pieces come and go. Much of the 2020 decor was temporary — don't build the day around one specific sign, and step aside for photos on Hakone-Yumoto's busy main street.
  • Volcanic areas close sometimes. Ōwakudani access is subject to volcanic activity alerts; follow posted restrictions.

Folding it into a Hakone trip

The pilgrimage traces the same loop every Hakone visitor already does, which makes this the easiest "secret second itinerary" in Japan:

  • Getting there: Odakyū line from Shinjuku to Hakone-Yumoto (Romancecar limited express ~85 min). The Hakone Free Pass covers the mountain railway, ropeway, buses and Lake Ashi boats — the pilgrimage runs entirely on it.
  • The classic loop is the Eva loop: Hakone-Yumoto (manholes, station shops) → mountain railway and cable car up → ropeway over Ōwakudani → down to Tōgendai → boat across Lake Ashi → Hakone Shrine's torii → bus back. Every leg passes something from the series.
  • Add Sengokuhara if you're staying overnight: the grasslands (spectacular in autumn), Kintoki Park's NERV outpost, and quiet onsen inns.
  • Stay in an onsen ryokan. Hot springs are canonically the one thing Tokyo-3 and real Hakone agree on completely. If you want to splurge, see our ryokan guide.

Common questions

Q. Is Hakone really Tokyo-3 from Evangelion?
A. Yes — the series explicitly sets its fortress city in the Hakone caldera, and the real geography (Lake Ashi, Sengokuhara, the ropeway) maps onto the fiction. The town leans into the connection with official Eva installations and tie-ups.

Q. Are there Evangelion manhole covers in Hakone?
A. Despite what some guides claim, we could not verify any Eva manhole covers in Hakone — none appear in official town materials or on-the-ground reports. Hakone's street-level Eva items are themed signboards, station decor and the NERV outpost. The confirmed Eva drain covers are Tokorozawa's solar-lit LED covers in Saitama.

Q. What is the NERV outpost in Hakone?
A. A public restroom in Kintoki Park, Sengokuhara, remodelled with NERV styling and AT-field patterning — an official, playful installation and a favourite photo stop.

Q. Can I visit the school from Evangelion?
A. The model was Sengokuhara Junior High School, which closed in 2008 and has since been repurposed. You can note the exterior from public ground, but the site is private — don't enter.

Q. How do I get to Hakone from Tokyo?
A. Odakyū line from Shinjuku to Hakone-Yumoto — about 85 minutes on the Romancecar limited express. The Hakone Free Pass covers the local transport loop (railway, cable car, ropeway, boats, buses) that doubles as the Eva route.

Q. Is one day enough?
A. The classic loop — Yumoto, ropeway, Tōgendai, Lake Ashi — fits in a long day trip. Add a night in an onsen ryokan if you want Sengokuhara and the NERV outpost without rushing.
