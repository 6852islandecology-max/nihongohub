How to Study for JLPT N5: A Roadmap That Actually Works

学 zero → N5

N5 is the first rung of the JLPT, and it is genuinely reachable for a beginner who studies a little most days. The mistake we see again and again is starting with grammar apps or kanji decks before the writing systems are solid. Learn to read kana first, and everything after it goes faster. Here is the order we would follow from a standing start, with the free official materials and a weekly loop you can keep.

We are writing this from inside a household that learns Japanese the slow way, around work and a kid. So the plan below assumes ordinary life, not a full-time language school. The point of having an order is that each step makes the next one cheaper: once you can sound out kana, vocabulary stops being meaningless squiggles, and once you have a few hundred words, grammar has something to attach to.

Quick answers

Q. How many months does it take to study for JLPT N5?
A. With steady daily study, most beginners reach a survival level of N5 in about 3 to 6 months, and a comfortable N5 into early N4 in roughly 6 to 12 months. The big variable is consistency: 30 to 45 minutes most days beats a long weekend cram.

Q. Can I self-study for N5 without a class or tutor?
A. Yes. N5 is the level most people self-study, because the content is well-mapped and there are free official sample questions to aim at. A tutor helps most with speaking and with checking your pronunciation, which is hard to self-correct.

Q. What order should I learn things in for N5?
A. Kana first (all 46 hiragana, then katakana), then basic vocabulary and the roughly 100 N5 kanji alongside grammar, then reading and listening practice. Save full practice tests for the last stretch, as a check rather than as your main material.

Q. Are there free materials for N5?
A. Yes. The official JLPT site publishes free sample questions and a list of reference materials for every level, including N5. Pair those with a free kana trainer and a free quiz to drill, and you can cover most of N5 before spending anything.

The roadmap in order

If you do nothing else, do these in this sequence. Each step assumes the one before it is mostly in place.

  1. Read kana. All 46 hiragana, then all 46 katakana, until you can read them without a chart. This is the gate to everything else.
  2. Build a core of vocabulary. A few hundred everyday words: numbers, time, family, food, common verbs and adjectives.
  3. Learn N5 kanji and grammar together. Around 100 kanji and the basic sentence patterns, side by side, so each reinforces the other.
  4. Train reading and listening. Short passages and slow audio, building up to the pace of the real test.
  5. Use practice tests as a check. In the final weeks, time yourself on official sample questions to find your weak spots.

Step 1: kana — hiragana first, then katakana

Japanese has two phonetic scripts of 46 basic characters each. Hiragana (ひらがな) is used for native words and grammar; katakana (カタカナ) is mostly for foreign loanwords and names. Learn hiragana first, because it appears in almost every sentence, then katakana right after. A motivated beginner can read both within a couple of weeks of short daily sessions.

Drill them by reading, not just by recognizing. Sound out simple words the moment you can, so the shapes connect to sounds and meaning instead of sitting as flashcards. We built a free kana trainer for exactly this loop. Practice hiragana and katakana free →

Don't sink months into writing kanji by hand for N5. Being able to read the characters is what the test checks, and reading is what unlocks the rest of your study. You can come back to handwriting later if you want it.

What N5 actually covers

N5 is meant to certify the ability to understand basic Japanese: simple sentences, set phrases, and everyday expressions written in kana and basic kanji. In rough numbers, the level is built on about 100 kanji and around 800 vocabulary words, plus the foundational grammar patterns. Those figures are the commonly used guideposts, not an official quota, so treat them as a target to aim for rather than a list to memorize exactly.

The grammar at N5 is the scaffolding of the language: は and が, basic particles, present and past, polite -ます forms, simple adjectives, and counting. It is small enough that you can hold the whole map in your head, which is part of why N5 is a satisfying first goal.

The three exam sections

The N5 test is split into three scored parts. Knowing the shape of it keeps your study balanced instead of all-vocabulary or all-grammar.

1. Language Knowledge, Vocabulary (文字・語彙 / moji-goi). Reading kana and kanji, and choosing the right word. This is where your kana fluency and your 800-word core pay off directly.

2. Language Knowledge, Grammar and Reading (文法・読解 / bunpō-dokkai). Picking the correct grammar form and understanding short passages, such as a sign, a note, or a simple paragraph. Grammar and reading are scored together here.

3. Listening (聴解 / chōkai). Short spoken exchanges and questions, played once. This is the section self-studiers tend to neglect, so build it in early rather than cramming it at the end.

A realistic timeline

Here is the honest version, based on steady study rather than a sprint. Reaching a survival level of N5 (reading kana fluently, handling basic exchanges, recognizing common kanji) usually takes about 3 to 6 months. Getting to a comfortable N5 and stepping into N4 territory is more like 6 to 12 months with consistent study. Faster timelines exist, but they almost always mean more hours per week rather than a secret method.

CONSISTENCY BEATS INTENSITY

The single best predictor of reaching N5 on schedule is showing up most days. Thirty to forty-five minutes daily, kept up for months, will carry you further than a three-hour Sunday that you skip half the time. Build the smallest habit you can actually keep, then add to it.

Booking a test date is a useful forcing function once you can read kana and have started on grammar. A deadline on the calendar turns a vague intention into a plan with weeks attached to it.

A week-by-week study loop

You do not need a 200-line plan. You need a small loop you repeat each week, with a little of each skill so nothing rots. A version that has worked for us, scaled to about 30 to 45 minutes a day:

  1. New input (2–3 days a week). A handful of new words and one new grammar point. Keep the batch small enough that you can actually recall it later.
  2. Review (every day). A few minutes of spaced review on what you have already seen. This is where the learning sticks, so it is the one part you never skip.
  3. Reading (2 days a week). Read something short out loud, like example sentences, a graded passage, or a quiz question, to tie kana, kanji, and grammar together.
  4. Listening (2 days a week). Slow audio you can mostly follow. Listen once for the gist, then again with the text.
  5. A quick check (weekly). A short quiz to see what stuck and what slipped, then feed the slips back into next week's review.

The two biggest stumbling blocks

Kanji. Even the roughly 100 kanji at N5 feel like a wall at first, because they are a new kind of symbol. The fix is to learn them as parts of words you already know, not as isolated characters. When you meet 食 inside 食べる (taberu, to eat), it has a hook to hang on. Learning kanji and vocabulary together, rather than as separate decks, is the single change that helps most.

Particles. The small words は, が, を, に, で, へ decide what a sentence means, and they rarely map one-to-one onto a word in your language. You will mix up は and が for a while, and that is normal. Get the common patterns through lots of example sentences instead of trying to memorize a rule for every case. The feel comes from exposure.

Where practice tests fit

Practice tests are a diagnostic, not the main course. Done too early, a full mock test just tells you what you already know, which is that you have not learned the material yet. Their real value comes in the last few weeks, when you use them to find specific weak spots and to get used to the timing and the question formats.

DON'T LET MOCK SCORES SET THE MOOD

An early practice test score is feedback on your study plan, not a verdict on you. If a section comes back weak, that is the section to feed more review into next week. Treat each mock as a map of what to do, then go back to your weekly loop.
