Why do moths and butterflies have "eyes" on their wings?

Touch a resting Japanese giant silk moth and its dull, bark-coloured forewings suddenly flick open to reveal two huge staring "eyes" underneath. It looks like a trick — and it is. Those markings are not eyes at all, just circles of scale on a wing. But the science of why this trick works, and why it isn't quite the trick biologists long assumed, has been rewritten by a large 2024 study. Here it is, told through Japan's own eyespotted insects. (We'll teach you the Japanese words as we go.)

Eyespots aren't warning colour — they're a different weapon entirely

It's easy to lump every flashy insect pattern into "warning colour," but biologists split this into two distinct strategies. Aposematism (warning colour, like a wasp's yellow-and-black) is an honest, permanent advertisement: "I am dangerous, remember my colour and avoid me next time." Deimatic display — from the Greek for "to frighten" — is the opposite kind of bluff: a normally hidden pattern that appears suddenly to startle a predator in the split second of an attack, buying the prey just enough time to escape. The insect isn't actually dangerous. It only needs to look alarming for about half a second.

DEIMATIC DISPLAY

A giant silk moth that hides snake eyes under its wings

臭酸 — kususan (Saturnia japonica) · 目玉模様 medama moyō (eyespot pattern, literally "eyeball pattern")

Kususan (Saturnia japonica) is one of Japan's largest moths, found from Hokkaido to Kyushu, with a wingspan that can pass 10 cm. At rest its forewings lie flat and camouflaged, mottled brown like dead leaves. But disturb it, and it snaps its forewings forward to expose a pair of large, dark, ringed eyespots on the hindwings — patterns that resemble a reptile's staring eyes. The moth doesn't run; it just sits there being suddenly, alarmingly "watched." For a bird that has learned to avoid snakes and lizards, that half-second of hesitation is exactly what the moth needs.

SEASONAL POLYPHENISM

The Okinawan butterfly with two different faces

アオタテハモドキ — ao-tatehamodoki (Junonia orithya, "blue false-nymphalid") · 季節型 kisetsu-gata (seasonal form)

Junonia orithya is a nymphalid butterfly established in Japan only in Okinawa Prefecture, though warming winters have pushed sightings north into the Amami islands and southern Kyushu in recent years. Like its wider genus, it shows striking seasonal polyphenism: the same genome builds a different wing pattern depending on the temperature and daylight the caterpillar experienced. Wet-season adults carry large, bold hindwing eyespots; dry-season adults shrink or nearly lose them, favouring drab camouflage instead. It's a live demonstration that a single species can switch between "startle the predator" and "don't be seen" as a strategy, simply by reading the season.

FALSE HEAD

A caterpillar wearing a snake's face

揚羽蝶 — ageha-chō (swallowtail butterfly) · 臭角 shūkaku (osmeterium, the caterpillar's forked "stink horn")

Young caterpillars of Japan's swallowtail butterflies (Papilio spp., ageha-chō) carry a pair of large false eye-markings on the swollen front segment of their body, making the whole head end look uncannily like a small snake's head. Provoked further, the caterpillar rears up and everts a bright orange, forked gland called the osmeterium (shūkaku) from just behind the "eyes," releasing a sharp citrus-like odour. European research on a related swallowtail confirmed this combination — the sudden eyespot reveal plus the osmeterium — measurably deters attacking birds, a textbook multi-sensory deimatic display.

The twist: eyespots may not need to look like eyes at all

For over a century the leading idea was "eye mimicry": eyespots work because predators (or predators' own predators) mistake them for the eyes of a large animal. A large 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis across dozens of experiments (eLife) found something more surprising — statistically, eyespots deterred predators no better than equally large, equally contrasty patterns that looked nothing like eyes. What predicted success instead was raw pattern size and boldness: bigger, high-contrast, single circular markings worked best, appearing to increase a predator's avoidance by roughly a fifth compared with plain controls, with paired or fragmented patterns performing worse than one strong circle. In short, the "eye" may be almost incidental — what actually startles a predator is anything sudden, big, and bold. Kususan's markings just happen to also look like a reptile's stare, which may be a bonus, not the whole mechanism.

The Japanese words you just learned: 臭酸 kususan (giant silk moth, Saturnia japonica), 目玉模様 medama moyō (eyespot pattern), アオタテハモドキ ao-tatehamodoki (Junonia orithya), 季節型 kisetsu-gata (seasonal form), 揚羽蝶 ageha-chō (swallowtail butterfly), 臭角 shūkaku (osmeterium).

Common questions

Q. What is a deimatic display?
A. A deimatic (startle) display is a normally hidden pattern or behaviour, such as an eyespot, that an animal suddenly reveals when attacked to frighten or momentarily confuse a predator, buying time to escape. It differs from aposematism (permanent warning colour) because the animal is not actually dangerous — it is bluffing.

Q. Why does the kususan moth (Saturnia japonica) have eyespots on its hindwings?
A. Its forewings are camouflaged at rest, but when disturbed it flicks them forward to reveal large ringed eyespots on the hindwings that resemble a reptile's staring eyes, startling would-be bird predators in the crucial split second of an attack.

Q. Does the Okinawan butterfly Junonia orithya always have the same eyespots?
A. No. It shows seasonal polyphenism: wet-season adults grow large, bold eyespots, while dry-season adults have smaller or reduced eyespots and rely more on camouflage, depending on the temperature and light the caterpillar experienced.

Q. Do swallowtail (ageha) caterpillars really mimic a snake's head?
A. Yes. Young Papilio caterpillars carry false eye-markings on a swollen front body segment, and when threatened they evert an orange, forked, foul-smelling gland called the osmeterium, combining a visual and a chemical startle defence.

Q. Do eyespots work because predators mistake them for real eyes?
A. A 2024 meta-analysis found no significant difference in deterrent effect between eyespots and equally large, equally bold non-eye patterns, suggesting general conspicuousness — not resemblance to a vertebrate eye — is the main driver, with larger single markings working best.
