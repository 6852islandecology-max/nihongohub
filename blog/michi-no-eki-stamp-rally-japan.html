Michi-no-eki stamp rally: Japan's roadside station stamps

UPDATED 2026-07 · NEW STATIONS REGISTERED EACH YEAR — CHECK THE OFFICIAL LIST

A road trip in Japan comes with its own collecting game. Every michi-no-eki (道の駅) — an official government-registered roadside station with local food, farm produce, and a rest area — keeps a free stamp, and many drivers plan whole routes around collecting them. There's a paid collectible too: the michi-no-eki kippu, a little train-ticket-style card for the station. This guide covers what the stations are, the free stamp, the kippu, how the road-trip stamp rally works, and the Japanese you'll use along the way.

What michi-no-eki are

A michi-no-eki (道の駅, literally "road station") is an official roadside rest stop registered by Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). The program started in 1993, and stations are numbered in the order they were designated. As of 2024 there were roughly 1,220 registered stations across every prefecture — treat any figure as a snapshot, because new ones are added each year.

Unlike a plain highway rest area, a michi-no-eki is built to show off its region: a farmers' market of local produce, a restaurant serving the area's speciality, souvenirs, clean 24-hour toilets and parking, and often a viewpoint or small attraction. That regional character is exactly what makes collecting them fun — each stop is a different corner of Japan.

The free stamp at each station

Every michi-no-eki has its own stamp (スタンプ), free to press, usually near the information counter or by the entrance. Like station rail stamps, each design features the station's name and something local — a mountain, a mascot, a famous crop. Collectors keep them in a dedicated stamp rally book so the pages build into a map of the roads they've driven.

  • Free and self-service — find the stamp, press it into your book, no purchase needed.
  • One per station — each registered station has its own unique design.
  • Bring a book — official regional stamp-rally books exist, but any notebook works.
⚠️ Check the hours

The parking and toilets are often 24-hour, but the shop and information area where the stamp lives keep normal opening hours. If you're arriving early or late, confirm the stamp is accessible — it may be indoors and locked when the building is closed.

The michi-no-eki kippu ticket

Separate from the free stamp, many stations sell a michi-no-eki kippu (道の駅きっぷ) — a small collectible card styled like an old railway ticket, printed with the station's name and often a date stamp. Unlike the free stamp, the kippu is a paid item you buy at the counter, and it has become its own collecting hobby with regional variants and special editions.

  • Paid — a small charge per ticket, bought at the station shop or counter.
  • Not everywhere — only participating stations issue them, so it's a smaller set than the free stamps.
  • A physical keepsake — the ticket-style card stores and displays neatly, like a rail-ticket collection.

Because the kippu is optional and station-specific, don't assume every michi-no-eki has one. If collecting the tickets is your goal, check which stations on your route participate before you set off.

How the stamp rally works

The free stamps turn naturally into a stamp rally (スタンプラリー). Regions issue rally books covering their michi-no-eki, and drivers aim to collect stamps from as many as they can; fill enough and you can submit the book for prizes or certificates in participating campaigns. Many people run rallies purely for the drive itself.

What you'll find there

Part of the appeal is that a stamp stop is also lunch, a snack, and a souvenir run. A typical michi-no-eki has:

  • A farmers' market — just-picked local produce, often cheaper and fresher than a supermarket.
  • A restaurant or food stand — the region's speciality dish, from soba to seafood to soft-serve in a local flavour.
  • Local souvenirs — area-only snacks and crafts you won't find in Tokyo.
  • Rest facilities — 24-hour parking and toilets, and sometimes a viewpoint, hot spring, or play area.

This makes michi-no-eki a driver's best friend even if you never collect a stamp — and a genuine reason to explore Japan's countryside by car rather than only its cities by train.

Common questions

Q. What is a michi-no-eki?
A. A michi-no-eki (道の駅, "road station") is an official roadside rest stop registered by Japan's MLIT since 1993. Each has a farmers' market, a restaurant, souvenirs, and 24-hour parking and toilets, and is built to showcase its region. As of 2024 there were around 1,220 registered stations nationwide.

Q. Are the michi-no-eki stamps free?
A. Yes. Each station has its own free, self-service stamp, usually near the information counter or entrance. You press it into your own notebook or a stamp-rally book — no purchase required.

Q. What is a michi-no-eki kippu?
A. It's a paid collectible card styled like an old railway ticket, printed with the station's name and often a date. Unlike the free stamp, you buy it at the counter, and only participating stations issue one, so it's a smaller collecting set.

Q. How does the stamp rally work?
A. Regions issue rally books covering their stations; you collect stamps from as many as you can, and completing a set can earn prizes or certificates in participating campaigns. Many people simply enjoy the drive.

Q. Can I collect stamps 24 hours a day?
A. Not always. Parking and toilets are often 24-hour, but the stamp is usually indoors near the shop or information area, which keeps normal hours. Check the opening times if you arrive early or late.

Q. Do I need a car?
A. Practically, yes — michi-no-eki are built for drivers and cluster along highways and country roads. Some are reachable by bus, but a rally really works as a road trip.
