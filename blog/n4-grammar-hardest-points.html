The N4 Grammar Wall: The Points That Trip Everyone (and the Tell for Each)

文 N4 · the grammar wall

N5 grammar mostly answers "what is this and where is it." N4 starts asking "who did what to whom, and how do you feel about it." That shift is where a lot of learners stall. The good news is that the wall is made of about eight predictable blocks, and each one has a tell: a small word or ending that gives the answer away once you know to look for it. We taught these to our own kids while traveling Japan, so the examples below are the plain, everyday kind you actually hear. Every sentence here is ours.

Quick answers

Q. What is the hardest N4 grammar?
A. For most learners it is the passive (受身), the causative (使役), and the causative-passive, because they change the verb ending and rearrange who did what to whom. The giving-and-receiving verbs (あげる・くれる・もらう) are a close second, since they encode direction and politeness that English handles with word order alone.

Q. How do I tell passive from causative?
A. Look at the ending and the feeling. Passive (e.g. 食べられる) means something was done to the subject, often unwanted. Causative (e.g. 食べさせる) means the subject made or let someone else do it. If there is a "made/let someone" idea, it is causative; if the subject is on the receiving end, it is passive.

Q. What is the difference between たら and ば?
A. Both mean "if," but たら leans on one specific completed event ("once X happens, then Y"), while ば states a more general or hypothetical condition ("if X is the case, Y"). たら can also describe a discovery ("when I did X, Y was already true"), which ば cannot.

Q. How do I self-study N4 grammar?
A. Learn one point at a time as a pattern plus its tell, write two or three of your own sentences with it, then drill mixed questions so you have to choose between points under pressure. Passive 受身: things happen to you

The passive takes a verb and points it back at the subject: someone or something does the action, and the subject is on the receiving end. You build it from the plain form. For る-verbs, replace る with られる (食べる → 食べられる). For う-verbs, change the final sound to its あ-row form and add れる (読む → 読まれる). In Japanese the passive often carries a faint sense that the event was unwanted or out of your control, which is why it shows up so much in stories about minor misfortunes.

PASSIVE EXAMPLE
弟に私のケーキを食べられました。
Otōto ni watashi no kēki o taberaremashita. — My cake got eaten by my little brother (and I am not happy about it).

Notice that the person doing the action is marked with に, and the subject (you) is the one affected. That little に is doing heavy lifting.

The tell: the doer is marked with に and the subject is the one things happen to. If you can paste "got [verb]-ed by ___" onto the English, you are in passive territory.

Causative 使役: making and letting

The causative says the subject caused someone else to do the action, either by making them (force) or letting them (permission). The endings mirror the passive but with せる instead of れる. For る-verbs: 食べる → 食べさせる. For う-verbs: 読む → 読ませる. Whether it means "make" or "let" comes from context and from the particle on the person doing the action.

One example

先生は学生に漢字を書かせました。
Sensei wa gakusei ni kanji o kakasemashita. — The teacher made the students write kanji.

The tell: the ending is …せる/…させる and there is a "made/let someone do it" meaning. The made-or-let person is marked with に: 先生は学生に漢字を書かせた. With an intransitive verb you may instead see を on that person: 母は子供を歩かせた (made the child walk).

Causative-passive: made to do it

This one stacks the two you just learned. The causative-passive means the subject was made to do something by someone else, usually unwillingly. You take the causative stem and put it into the passive: 書かせる → 書かせられる, which in everyday speech for う-verbs often shortens to 書かされる. The feeling is "I had to do this and I did not want to."

One example

子供のとき、毎日ピアノを練習させられました。
Kodomo no toki, mainichi piano o renshū saseraremashita. — When I was a child, I was made to practice piano every day.

The tell: the ending is …せられる/…させられる, and the subject is both the doer and the unwilling one. う-verbs (not す-ending ones) also have a short form: 書かせられる → 書かされる (kakasareru); 話す has no short form. Read it as "was made to ___."

Giving and receiving: あげる・くれる・もらう

English uses one verb, "give," and lets word order sort out the direction. Japanese uses three verbs, and the choice depends on who is giving to whom relative to you. This is why learners freeze: the grammar is fine, but the perspective is new.

あげる is for giving away from your side: you give to someone, or one outside person gives to another. くれる is for giving toward your side: someone gives to you or your group. もらう is receiving: you (or your side) get something, and the giver is marked with に or から.

GIVING & RECEIVING EXAMPLE
友達が私に辞書をくれました。
Tomodachi ga watashi ni jisho o kuremashita. — My friend gave me a dictionary.

Try the same event with もらう and the camera moves to you: 私は友達に辞書をもらいました (Watashi wa tomodachi ni jisho o moraimashita, "I received a dictionary from my friend"). Same dictionary, different verb, because the subject changed.

The tell: ask which way the thing is moving. Toward you or your group, the verb is くれる. Away from you, あげる. If the subject is the one receiving, it is もらう, and the giver wears に or から.

The four conditionals: と・ば・たら・なら

N4 hands you four ways to say "if" or "when," and they are not freely swappable. Here is the short version of when each one fits.

と — automatic results

このボタンを押すと、ドアが開きます。
Kono botan o osu to, doa ga akimasu. — If/when you press this button, the door opens.

The tell: use と for natural, automatic, "always happens" results, like machines and directions. The second part cannot be a command or a request.

ば — general or hypothetical conditions

時間があれば、手伝います。
Jikan ga areba, tetsudaimasu. — If I have time, I will help.

The tell: use ば for a general "if this is true, then that" statement, often hypothetical. It focuses on the condition itself.

たら — once one event finishes

家に帰ったら、電話します。
Uchi ni kaettara, denwa shimasu. — Once I get home, I will call you.

The tell: use たら for one specific event happening first, then the next thing. It is the most flexible "if/when," and the only one that comfortably leads into a command or request.

なら — "if that is the case" / on the topic of

京都に行くなら、お寺を見たほうがいいです。
Kyōto ni iku nara, otera o mita hō ga ii desu. — If you are going to Kyoto, you should see the temples.

The tell: use なら to respond to something just said or assumed, meaning "given that, here is my advice/comment." It often reacts to the other person's statement.

THE FAST WAY TO CHOOSE

When a question forces you to pick, run this order. Is it an automatic, unavoidable result (machines, nature, directions)? と. Is it advice or a comment reacting to what was just said? なら. Does one event clearly finish before the next, or could the result be a request or command? たら. Otherwise, for a plain general condition, ば is safe. Drilling mixed sentences is the only way this becomes fast.

Potential form: being able to

The potential form says you can do something. For る-verbs, 食べる → 食べられる (yes, it looks like the passive, and context separates them). For う-verbs, change the final sound to its え-row form and add る: 話す → 話せる, 読む → 読める. する becomes できる, and 来る becomes 来られる. One pattern to watch: the thing you are able to do usually takes が, not を.

One example

私はひらがなが読めます。
Watashi wa hiragana ga yomemasu. — I can read hiragana.

The tell: the meaning is "can/able to," and the object switches from を to が. With る-verbs it shares its shape with the passive, so let the sentence's meaning decide which one it is.

Must, may, and must not

Three patterns cover obligation, permission, and prohibition, and they all attach to a verb form you already know. They feel long at first because the "must" pattern is built from a double negative, but the shape is fixed and you can memorize it as one chunk.

~なければならない (must / have to): take the ない-form, drop the final ない, and add なければならない (行かない → 行かなければならない). 行く → 行かなければならない (must go). In casual speech it often shortens to 行かなきゃ.

~てもいい (may / it is okay to): take the て-form and add もいい. 入ってもいいです (you may come in).

~てはいけない (must not): take the て-form and add はいけない. 入ってはいけません (you must not enter). Casually this contracts to ~ちゃいけない.

One example

明日は早く起きなければなりません。
Ashita wa hayaku okinakereba narimasen. — Tomorrow I have to get up early.

The tell: match the ending to the job. なければならない / なきゃ = must do it. てもいい = allowed. てはいけない / ちゃだめ = not allowed. The "must" one is the long one because it literally says "if not, it won't do."

Looks like vs I heard: そう・らしい

Here learners lose points to a spelling coincidence. There are two different ~そう, plus ~らしい, and they report information in different ways.

~そう (appearance): attaches to the verb stem or adjective stem and means "it looks like / seems about to." It is your own guess from what you see. 雨が降りそうです (it looks like it is going to rain, from the dark clouds you see).

~そう (hearsay): attaches to the plain form and means "I heard that." It passes along what someone told you. 雨が降るそうです (I heard it is going to rain, because the forecast said so).

~らしい: also reports secondhand information, but with a sense that it is based on reliable evidence or general reputation. 田中さんは来ないらしいです (it seems Tanaka isn't coming, from what I gather).

The two そう side by side

おいしそうです。／ おいしいそうです。
Oishisō desu. (It looks delicious — I can see it.) / Oishii sō desu. (I heard it is delicious — someone told me.)

The tell: check what comes before そう. Stem + そう (おいしそう) is your own eyes, "looks like." Plain form + そう (おいしいそう) is hearsay, "I heard." For らしい, the source is someone else but you trust it.

How to study this without drowning

The mistake we see most is trying to learn all eight blocks in one sitting and then mixing them up on the test. A better loop has three steps. First, learn one point as a pattern plus its tell, the way each block above is laid out. Second, write two or three of your own sentences with it, about your real day, so the pattern attaches to something concrete. Third, drill mixed questions, where you have to choose between points, because the exam never tells you which one is coming.

Treat practice questions as a diagnosis, not the lesson. When you miss one, the value is in seeing which tell you ignored, then going back to that block. If you want the format the real exam uses, work through the official JLPT N4 sample questions linked below before you sit it.
