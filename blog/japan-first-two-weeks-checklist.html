Your First 14 Days in Japan: An Arrival Checklist in the Right Order

着 arrival · 着いてから

You have your residence card (or you're about to). Now a stack of errands stands between you and a working life in Japan, and they all seem to depend on each other. The trick is the order. This is the arrival checklist in the sequence that actually works, so the phone-needs-a-bank-needs-an-address loop never traps you. The errands themselves are not hard. What makes the first two weeks stressful is that nobody hands you the order, so people try to open a bank account on day two, get turned away for having no registered address, and lose a week. Do them in the sequence below and each step unlocks the next. We re-ran this list against our own move and the relocation timeline tool to make sure nothing here sends you in a circle.

Quick answers

Q. What must I do in my first 14 days in Japan?
A. The one hard legal deadline is registering your address (転入届 / tennyū-todoke) at your local city hall within 14 days of moving in. While you're there you also get enrolled in My Number and, if your employer doesn't cover you, in National Health Insurance and the National Pension. Collecting your residence card and opening a bank account and SIM follow from that.

Q. What order should I do everything in?
A. Residence card at the airport, then city hall address registration (which prints your address onto the card), then insurance and pension at the same counter, then a bank account, then a local SIM. Each step needs the document the previous step produced, so doing them out of order gets you turned away.

Q. What should I prepare before landing?
A. Have your signed lease (it usually works as proof of address at city hall), your passport, your Certificate of Eligibility paperwork, some yen in cash, and an eSIM activated so you have data the moment you land. Learning to read basic kana also helps, because city hall forms use them.

Q. Bank account or phone first?
A. Neither is truly first. Register your address at city hall first, because most banks and SIM contracts ask for a residence card, and having your registered address printed on it makes every case go smoothly. An eSIM bridges the no-phone gap until you have the local SIM.

Before you land: what to sort first

A few minutes of prep on the plane side of the trip removes most of the friction on the Japan side. None of this is paperwork you file in advance; it is the documents and data you'll want in hand the moment you walk out of the airport.

PRE-ARRIVAL CHECKLIST

  • Signed lease in hand — it usually works as proof of address at city hall
  • Passport and your Certificate of Eligibility / visa paperwork
  • Your employer or school's contact for the documents they handle
  • Some yen in cash for the first few days (cards aren't accepted everywhere)
  • An eSIM activated so you have data the instant you land
  • Basic kana practice — city hall forms are in Japanese

The 14-day sequence at a glance

DO THEM IN THIS ORDER

  • 1. Collect your residence card at airport immigration
  • 2. Register your address at city hall within 14 days (+ My Number)
  • 3. Enroll in National Health Insurance and pension (same visit, if not employer-covered)
  • 4. Open a bank account (Japan Post is the most newcomer-friendly)
  • 5. Get a local SIM

Each number below needs the document the previous number produced. The full reasoning, premiums, and edge cases live in our pillar, the practical moving-to-Japan timeline. This page is just the running order.

STEP 1Day 1 — Collect your residence card

If you land at a major airport such as Narita, Haneda, Kansai, Chubu and a growing list of others, immigration issues your residence card (在留カード / zairyū kādo) at the counter on arrival; at smaller ports the card is mailed to your registered address. The address field is blank for now; city hall fills it in at Step 2. This card is your main ID in Japan, so keep it on you. Full detail on categories and the card itself is in our pillar guide. Official source: Immigration Services Agency.

STEP 2Days 1–14 — Register your address, and get My Number

This is the only step with a legal deadline. Within 14 days of moving into your home, file your move-in registration (転入届 / tennyū-todoke) at your local city, ward, or town hall. Bring your residence card, your passport, and your lease. The clerk prints your registered address onto the back of your residence card, which is the document everything else in this list asks for. You're also enrolled in My Number, Japan's 12-digit national ID; the notification arrives by post a few weeks later. Sources: Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications · My Number portal.

PHRASE: AT CITY HALL
転入届を出したいのですが。
Tennyū-todoke o dashitai no desu ga. — I'd like to file my move-in registration.
THE CHICKEN-AND-EGG TRAP

The landlord wants a residence card before signing, but your card only shows an address after city hall, and city hall wants proof of address. The way out: most city halls accept a signed lease as proof of address for this registration, and most landlords sign on the strength of the card you already collected at the airport. Confirm with your agent before you arrive so the order holds. The bank and SIM steps both wait on this card being updated.

STEP 3Same visit — Health insurance and pension

Do this at the same counter while you're already at city hall. If your employer covers you through company insurance (社会保険 / shakai hoken), it's automatic and you can skip ahead. If you're self-employed, freelancing, a student, or between jobs, enroll in National Health Insurance (国民健康保険) and the National Pension (国民年金) here. Premiums, coverage percentages, and the lump-sum pension withdrawal for when you leave are all covered in the pillar. The point for sequencing is simply that it happens on the same trip as Step 2, so you don't make a second visit.

PHRASE: AT THE COUNTER
国民健康保険に加入したいのですが。
Kokumin kenkō hoken ni kanyū shitai no desu ga. — I'd like to enroll in National Health Insurance.

STEP 4Then — Open a bank account

Now that your residence card shows a registered address, a bank will deal with you. Japan Post Bank (ゆうちょ銀行 / Yūcho Ginkō) is the most reliably newcomer-friendly choice, because every post office branch has a counter and the residency bar is low. Bring your residence card with the printed address and your passport. Many of the big commercial banks ask for six months of residency first, so Japan Post or a newcomer-friendly online bank is the safer opening move. Which banks accept newcomers, and the SIM that pairs well with them, is laid out in our moving-to-Japan guide.

STEP 5Then — Get a local SIM

A local Japanese number is the last lock to open, and by now you have what it needs: a residence card with an address and a bank or card to pay with. Budget MVNOs like IIJmio, Mineo, and Rakuten Mobile are cheap with some English support; the big carriers cost more for wider coverage. Your arrival eSIM keeps you online in the meantime, so you're never stranded without data while the local SIM is set up. The bank-and-SIM specifics are in our moving-to-Japan guide.

FIRST 14 DAYS — DONE WHEN

  • Residence card collected at the airport
  • Address registered at city hall within 14 days, card updated
  • Enrolled in National Health Insurance and pension (if not employer-covered)
  • Bank account opened (Japan Post or newcomer-friendly bank)
  • Local SIM active, arrival eSIM retired

Why this order breaks the loop

The famous chicken-and-egg problem is real, but it only bites if you start in the wrong place. The two things that quietly break the circle are a signed lease and an eSIM. The lease usually counts as proof of address for the city hall step, which means you don't need a registered address to register your address. The eSIM gives you data and a way to reach landlords and banks before you hold a Japanese number, which removes the no-phone gap from the very start. With those two in hand, the rest is a straight line: card, address, insurance, bank, SIM.
