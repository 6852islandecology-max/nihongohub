How to watch sumo morning practice in Tokyo

Morning practice — asageiko (朝稽古) — is sumo without the ceremony: wrestlers drilling, colliding and collapsing two meters in front of you, in silence broken only by breathing and the slap of skin. Many visitors rate it above the tournament itself. But here's what most articles gloss over: sumo stables are not tourist attractions. They're live-in workplaces, most no longer accept casual visitors, and the ones that do can stop at any time. This guide covers the one stable that officially welcomes free walk-up viewing, when a paid tour genuinely earns its fee, and the etiquette that keeps these doors open at all. And if you're here in mid-September — the Tokyo tournament runs September 13–27 this year, so you can do both.

The reality of stable visits in 2026

A generation ago, politely calling a stable and asking to watch practice sometimes worked. Today most stables have closed to casual visitors — practice is work, foreign visitors without a Japanese speaker were a recurring problem, and a viral incident is a bigger risk to a stable than a tourist's goodwill is a benefit. What remains are three honest routes:

The free option: Arashio stable

Arashio-beya in central Tokyo is the famous exception: it officially welcomes the public to watch morning practice from outside the building, through its street-facing windows. From the stable's own English-language visitor page, the terms are:

  • Practice is viewable roughly 7:30–9:00am; no reservation needed, and it's free.
  • The schedule is not guaranteed — practice moves or stops around tournaments and regional tours. The stable maintains a public calendar on its website; check it the night before.
  • House rules: keep quiet, no flash photography, no eating or drinking, and don't sit on the neighbors' steps.

Treat it like the gift it is: this is a stable voluntarily sharing its working morning with strangers, and the arrangement survives only as long as visitors behave. Confirm the current rules and calendar on the stable's official site (arashio.net) before you go.

When a guided tour is worth the money

The honest calculus: if you just want to see practice and you're comfortable standing on a street at 7:30am, Arashio's window costs nothing. A paid tour earns its fee in three situations — you want to sit inside the practice room rather than watch through glass; you want someone to explain what the drills, hierarchy and rituals actually mean (asageiko is far richer with commentary); or your dates don't line up with Arashio's calendar and a tour's arrangements with other stables are your way in. Some tours add breakfast of chanko-nabe, the wrestlers' stew, afterwards.

Check the cancellation terms when you book: morning practice can be cancelled by the stable on short notice (that's the nature of watching a real workplace), and the better tours say clearly what happens to your booking when it is.

Etiquette — read this before you go

  • Silence is the default. No talking beyond a whisper, phones on silent. The Arashio rules say it plainly: keep quiet.
  • No flash, ever. Photos without flash are generally tolerated at the window; inside a stable on a tour, follow your guide's instructions exactly.
  • No eating, drinking or smoking while watching.
  • Don't block the street or the neighbors. The stable sits in a residential neighborhood; don't sit on private steps, and keep the pavement passable.
  • Arrive early, leave quietly. Practice intensity builds toward the end of the session — arriving at 8am beats arriving at 7:29 and leaving at 7:45.
  • Take the cancellation gracefully. If there's no practice the day you show up, that's the deal with a free window into a real workplace.

Pair it with the September tournament

If you're in Tokyo in mid-September, you can see both sides of sumo in one trip: morning practice early, the real thing at Ryogoku Kokugikan later. From the Japan Sumo Association's official schedule, the 2026 September tournament (Aki basho) runs Sunday, September 13 to Sunday, September 27 at Kokugikan, with advance tickets on sale from August 8 through the JSA's official ticket channels. Popular days — weekends and the final days — sell out first; weekday tickets are the realistic walk-up-era option. Buy from official channels: Japan's ticket resale law makes unauthorized above-face-value resale illegal, and marked-up tickets can be refused at the door.

Common questions

Q. Can you watch sumo morning practice for free?
A. Yes — Arashio stable in central Tokyo officially welcomes the public to watch practice from the street through its windows, roughly 7:30–9:00am, free and without reservation. Practice isn't guaranteed daily, so check the stable's official calendar the night before.

Q. Do I need a tour to see a sumo stable?
A. Not for Arashio's window. A tour is worth it if you want to sit inside the practice room, want expert commentary on what you're seeing, or need access on dates when the free option isn't running.

Q. Can I just knock on a sumo stable's door?
A. Don't. Most stables no longer accept casual visitors; the traditional route requires advance permission and normally a Japanese speaker. Showing up unannounced is exactly the behavior that closes doors for everyone.

Q. When is the Tokyo sumo tournament in 2026?
A. The September tournament runs September 13–27, 2026 at Ryogoku Kokugikan (official JSA schedule), with advance tickets on sale from August 8. January and May tournaments are also held in Tokyo.
