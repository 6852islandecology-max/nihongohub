Japanese whetstones explained: King vs Shapton vs Suehiro vs Naniwa

You bought (or covet) a Japanese kitchen knife, and now the internet insists you need a whetstone — but every thread argues about brands, grits and soaking times, and half the advice assumes you already know what #1000 means. This guide untangles it: what grit numbers actually do, how Japan's four big synthetic-stone brands genuinely differ, and the story behind the natural stones quarried around Kyoto that sharpeners have prized for eight centuries. This is an editorial comparison built on manufacturer specs and long-running public user feedback — no single stone is right for everyone, and each pick below comes with its honest downside.

How to choose: three things that actually matter

1. Grit (番手) — the number decides the job. Low numbers are coarse, high numbers are fine. Roughly: #220–400 repairs chips and reshapes; #800–1200 is the everyday sharpening zone where a dull knife becomes sharp; #3000–8000 refines that edge into a cleaner, longer-lasting one. If you own one stone, make it a #1000. A finishing stone is the upgrade, not the starting point.

2. Hard and fast vs soft and forgiving. Synthetic stones trade speed against feedback. Softer stones (King is the classic) feel smooth, show you what's happening, and forgive sloppy angles — but wear hollow faster and cut slower on hard modern steels. Harder ceramic stones (Shapton is the reference point) cut quickly and stay flat, but feel less communicative and are less forgiving of a wobbly stroke. Beginners usually learn better on softer; impatient sharpeners and hard "super steels" favor harder.

3. Soak or splash. Traditional waterstones want a 10–15 minute soak before use; splash-and-go stones need only a wet surface. If you know a soaking ritual will stop you sharpening at all, that alone is a reason to pay more for splash-and-go.

The stones, honestly reviewed

King KDS 1000/6000 — the classic first stone

King (Matsunaga) stones are what generations of Japanese households learned on, and the KDS combination brick — #1000 on one face, #6000 on the other — is still the standard recommendation for a first setup. It's a soft, traditional soaking waterstone: give the #1000 face 10–15 minutes in water (the #6000 face only needs a splash) and it sharpens with a smooth, tactile feedback that tells your hands what the edge is doing. Public ratings consistently hover around 4.6 stars, and the recurring theme is the same — it makes learning feel possible. The flip side of soft is honest too: it wears hollow faster than harder stones and you'll need to flatten it regularly, and on very hard powder steels it cuts slowly.

✔ Forgiving feedback for learners · both core grits in one brick · decades-proven

✘ Needs soaking before every session · dishes faster, so budget for a flattening plate · slow on very hard modern steels

Best for: first-time sharpeners with ordinary kitchen knives who want one purchase that covers sharpening and finishing. Skip it if: you hate the soaking ritual or your knives are hard "super steel" — look at Shapton instead.

Shapton Ha No Kuromaku #1000 + #5000 — hard, fast, no soaking

Shapton's Ha No Kuromaku ("the blade's kuromaku" — literally the black curtain, the power working behind the scenes; sold as Shapton Pro overseas) is the opposite philosophy: hard ceramic stones that need only a splash of water, cut noticeably faster than traditional waterstones, and hold their flatness far longer. The two-stone set pairs the orange #1000 with the wine #5000, and each stone's plastic case doubles as a sharpening stand. Professionals and impatient home cooks gravitate here. The honest trade-off: the hard surface gives less tactile feedback, which punishes an unsteady angle, and the set costs meaningfully more than a beginner combination stone.

✔ Splash-and-go — zero soaking · fast cutting even on hard steels · stays flat longer · cases double as stands

✘ Less feedback for learners · pricier than entry stones · hard surface magnifies technique errors

Best for: anyone who sharpens often, owns harder Japanese steel, or knows soaking would kill the habit. Skip it if: you're on a tight budget or you're still finding your angle — a softer stone teaches more gently.

Suehiro Cerax #6000 — the dedicated polisher

Suehiro has made sharpening stones in Niigata since the 1950s, and the Cerax line's #6000 is a specialist: it doesn't sharpen a dull knife, it refines a sharp one. After a #1000 session, a few minutes on the Cerax leaves a cleaner, more polished edge that glides through tomatoes and holds up longer between sharpenings. Users consistently praise its creamy, consistent feel. Be clear about what it is not: on its own it will barely move a dull edge, and like most finishing waterstones it's on the softer side, so it needs occasional flattening.

✔ Noticeably cleaner edge after a #1000 stone · smooth, consistent feel · trusted maker

✘ Useless as your only stone — it finishes, it doesn't sharpen · soft enough to need flattening · brief pre-wetting still recommended

Best for: someone who already owns and uses a #1000 and wants the next level of edge. Skip it if: you don't own a medium stone yet — buy that first, always.

Naniwa Gouken #3000 (NA-0300) — the affordable middle step

Osaka-based Naniwa makes everything from budget bricks to the luxury Chosera line; this stone from the Gouken Arata (剛研 新) professional finishing series is the value pick. #3000 is a genuinely useful grit — fine enough to leave a kitchen-ready edge straight off the stone, coarse enough to follow a #1000 without fuss — and this is one of the cheapest respectable ways to get it. The compromises are physical: it's a slimmer, lighter stone than premium lines, so it will wear through sooner, and at this price the feel is serviceable rather than luxurious. As a first "second stone", that maths works.

✔ Wallet-friendly entry to fine grits · #3000 is a practical do-it-all finishing point for kitchen knives · light and easy to store

✘ Thinner stone = shorter total lifespan · plainer feel than premium lines · enthusiasts will outgrow it

Best for: upgrading from a single #1000 on a small budget, or anyone unsure whether fine finishing is worth it to them. Skip it if: you already know you love sharpening — buy a thicker premium stone once instead of this twice.

Hideriyama awasedo — a natural finishing stone from Kyoto

The four stones above are engineered products; this one was made by the planet. Hideriyama is a quarry area near Kameoka, Kyoto, and an awasedo (合砥) is a natural finishing stone cut from its strata — the same family of Kyoto stones that sword polishers and carpenters have sought since the Kamakura period. Instead of a grit number you get fine seabed sediment laid down roughly 200–250 million years ago and later compressed into ultra-fine-grained rock: particles that break down as you work, leaving the hazy, misty kasumi finish on carbon-steel knives that synthetics struggle to imitate. The honest caveats are real: every natural stone is unique, so hardness and fineness vary piece to piece; there's a learning curve; and an entry natural stone costs more than most complete synthetic setups while being objectively less predictable. You buy it for the experience and the finish, not for efficiency.

✔ The classic kasumi finish on carbon steel · every stone one of a kind · a working piece of Kyoto's craft heritage

✘ No standardised grit — quality varies stone to stone · steeper learning curve · costs more and cuts slower than synthetics of similar fineness

Best for: enthusiasts with solid technique, carbon-steel knife owners chasing a kasumi finish, or a genuinely meaningful gift for a knife person. Skip it if: you want predictable, repeatable results — that is exactly what synthetics were invented for.

Why Kyoto? The strata behind the legend

Japan's most famous natural whetstones come from a narrow band of geology around the Kyoto basin and the Tamba highlands. The short version: hundreds of millions of years ago, extremely fine sediment — much of it the silica skeletons of plankton — settled on a deep seabed, was compressed into ultra-fine-grained rock, and was later folded into the hills northwest of Kyoto as the land buckled. Miners found that stones from certain seams sharpened steel like nothing else, and from the Kamakura period onward quarries with now-hallowed names — Nakayama, Ōhira, Narutaki, Shōbudani, Hideriyama — supplied the sword polishers, carpenters and cooks of the capital. The finest finishing stones from these seams, the awasedo, became a currency of the craft world.

Most of those mines are now closed or barely active, which is why good natural stones keep getting scarcer and pricier — each one sold today is, in a real sense, non-renewable. If the geology hooks you, Kameoka — the town on the old strata just west of Kyoto city — runs the Tennen Toishikan (天然砥石館), a small museum of natural whetstones where you can see stones from the historic quarries and even try sharpening on them. Common questions

Q. What grit whetstone do I need first?
A. A #1000 medium stone. It turns a dull knife sharp, which is the whole job. Finishing stones (#3000–8000) refine an edge that already exists, and coarse stones (#220–400) are for chips and repairs — both are add-ons, not starting points.

Q. King KDS or Shapton Ha No Kuromaku — which should a beginner buy?
A. King if you want soft, forgiving feedback and don't mind soaking the stone first; Shapton if you want splash-and-go convenience, faster cutting and better performance on hard steels, at a higher price. Most people who quit sharpening quit over friction — pick whichever removes yours.

Q. Do Japanese whetstones need to be soaked?
A. Traditional waterstones like the King KDS do — about 10–15 minutes until bubbles stop. Splash-and-go stones like Shapton's Ha No Kuromaku line just need water on the surface. Never use oil on a Japanese waterstone.

Q. What is an awasedo?
A. Awasedo (合砥) are the finest grade of Japanese natural finishing stones, classically quarried from the strata around Kyoto — mines like Nakayama, Ōhira and Hideriyama. They have no standardised grit; each stone is unique, and they're prized for the hazy kasumi finish they leave on carbon steel.

Q. Are natural whetstones better than synthetic ones?
A. Not for most people. Synthetics are cheaper, faster and consistent; naturals are variable, slower and cost more, but produce a finish and feel enthusiasts love — and the closed Kyoto mines mean each stone is a small piece of a finite heritage. Learn on synthetic, romance later.

Q. Can I visit where Kyoto's whetstones come from?
A. Yes — Kameoka, just west of Kyoto city, has the Tennen Toishikan (Natural Whetstone Museum), where you can see stones from the historic quarries and try sharpening on natural stones.
