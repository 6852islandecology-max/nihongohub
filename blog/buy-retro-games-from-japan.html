Buying retro games from Japan

Japan kept its games. Decades of careful ownership — boxes, manuals, dust sleeves — mean the Japanese second-hand market is the deepest, best-preserved source of retro games on earth, from Famicom and Super Famicom through Saturn, Dreamcast and PS2. Japanese copies of the same game usually cost less than their Western releases, and Japan-only titles never had a Western release at all. Toys and hobby goods are the fastest-growing category in Japan's cross-border e-commerce (BEENOS 2025 ranking), and retro games are a big reason why. Here's where the games actually live, how to read listings, and the pitfalls — region locks, voltage, dead save batteries, fake carts — that catch first-time importers.

Why import from Japan

  • Condition culture. Japanese collectors kept boxes and manuals. Complete-in-box copies that are rare and expensive in the West are ordinary stock in Japan.
  • Price. For most titles, the Japanese version is the cheaper one — sometimes dramatically so for games that are common in Japan but scarce abroad.
  • Japan-only libraries. Whole genres — visual novels, rhythm games, many RPGs — never left Japan. For Famicom Disk System, Virtual Boy or Japanese computer games (PC-98, MSX), Japan is the only market.
  • Language barrier works in your favor. Action, racing, shmup and fighting games play fine without Japanese; text-heavy RPGs are the ones to research first.

Region locks, voltage, batteries and fakes

  • Region compatibility. Japanese games are NTSC-J. Some systems and games run on Western hardware with adapters or mods, others don't — check compatibility for your specific console before buying a stack of carts. Buying a cheap Japanese console alongside the games sidesteps the whole question.
  • Voltage. Japanese consoles are built for Japan's 100V outlets. In 110–120V regions many survive, in 220–240V regions you need a step-down converter — or you'll cook a working Famicom. Factor a converter into the budget.
  • Save batteries. Cartridge save batteries from the 80s–90s are dying of old age regardless of country. Assume a battery-backed cart will need a battery swap sooner or later; it doesn't make the game worthless, just budget for it.
  • Fake carts. Reproduction cartridges cluster around expensive, popular titles (Pokémon and other high-value carts especially). Original-photo listings, established sellers and 動作確認済み wording help; a rare title at a too-good price from a fresh account is the classic red flag.
  • US buyers: duties. The US ended its de minimis exemption in 2025, so US-bound parcels can owe import duties regardless of value.

Common questions

Q. Are retro games cheaper in Japan?
A. Usually, yes — Japan's supply of well-kept copies is deep, so Japanese versions of most titles cost less than Western ones, and complete-in-box condition is far more common. Japan-only titles obviously have no Western price to compare.

Q. Will Japanese games work on my console?
A. It depends on the system. Japanese games are NTSC-J and some hardware is region-locked; adapters and mods exist for many systems. Check compatibility for your exact console first, or buy a Japanese console with the games.

Q. What does ジャンク (junk) mean in listings?
A. Sold as-is: untested or known faulty, no returns. Junk lots on Yahoo! Auctions are how resellers and tinkerers buy cheap in bulk — great value if you can repair, a gamble if you can't.
