Japanese train sets: Plarail or KATO N gauge, from the Shinkansen to the Yamanote Line

UPDATED 2026-11 · EDITORIAL COMPARISON BASED ON MAKERS' SPECIFICATIONS, NOT SPONSORED PICKS

You rode the Shinkansen, or your child has watched every Hayabusa video online, and now you want a Japanese train at home. Searching for one gives you two very different things. One is a blue plastic toy track with a chunky battery-powered bullet train. The other is a box of finely detailed carriages that need separate track, a power pack and some patience. Both are "Japanese train sets", but they are made for different people and their parts are not interchangeable. This guide explains the three choices that matter, matches the model trains to the real routes they run on, and covers five products, two toys and three hobby items, with the downsides of each. It is an editorial comparison based on the manufacturers' published specifications. We have not tested these products ourselves.

How to choose: three things that actually matter

1. Toy or scale model. Plarail (プラレール) is Takara Tomy's long-running toy railway: plastic track and plastic trains that run on household batteries carried inside the train. There is no wiring and no transformer, and children can build and rearrange the layout themselves. Takara Tomy labels Plarail for ages 3 and up. N gauge (Nゲージ) is a scale-model hobby. The trains run on 9 mm metal-railed track and take their power from the rails, supplied by a separate power pack. The detail is far higher, and so is the care they need. The two systems are not compatible: a Plarail train cannot run on N gauge track, and the reverse is also true.

2. Which scale "N" means. N gauge always runs on 9 mm track, but the scale differs. Japanese N gauge models of ordinary narrow-gauge trains, such as the Yamanote Line E235, are built to 1:150. Shinkansen models, and most N scale models made outside Japan, are built to 1:160, because the real Shinkansen runs on wider standard-gauge track. Both run on the same 9 mm track. The practical point is that if you later mix a Japanese commuter train with an American or European N scale model, they can look slightly mismatched in size even though they share the rails.

3. Your mains voltage. This is the most common expensive mistake. Japan's household electricity is 100 V, and power packs sold for the Japanese market are designed for it. The N gauge train never plugs into the wall; the power pack does, and that is the part that has to suit your country. Do not plug a Japanese domestic power pack straight into a 120 V or 230 V outlet. Buy a controller sold for your own mains supply instead, such as the power pack in Kato USA's North American starter set below, or an N scale controller from a local shop. Plarail does not have this problem because it runs on batteries, which is one reason it is the easier gift to send abroad.

The real trains, and how far they take you

Each model on this page is a copy of a train that is running in Japan today, so it helps to know where the real one goes. The E5 series Hayabusa is the green-and-white train on the Tohoku Shinkansen north from Tokyo. The N700S is the current flagship of the Tokaido and Sanyo Shinkansen, running the fastest Nozomi services west. The E235 series is the green-striped commuter train on the Yamanote Line, the loop around central Tokyo. It is the train whose station jingles we cover in our Yamanote Line departure melodies guide. The five sets, honestly reviewed

Plarail entry set, E5 Hayabusa (214366): the simplest start, if you can still find it

This is the simplest version of the toy: an E5 Hayabusa Plarail train with a basic loop of blue Plarail track. The child puts the track together, switches the train on and watches it run, with batteries supplying the power. Takara Tomy labels Plarail for ages 3 and up. The format's main strength is that it grows: Plarail track, stations and other trains are sold separately, so a starter loop can turn into a room-sized layout over the years. The main problem is availability. When we checked on 6 November 2026, the amazon.com listing said only one was left in stock, so it may sell out or come back at a different price. A basic loop also gets dull fast. Most families end up buying extra track and pieces quite soon.

✔ No wiring or power pack, so the voltage question doesn't arise · aimed by the maker at ages 3+ · expandable with the wider Plarail range · the real E5 is a recognisable train to take home after a Tohoku trip

✘ Stock on amazon.com was down to one when we checked, so availability is uncertain · a basic loop needs extra track to stay interesting · a toy, not a scale model, so adults wanting detail will be disappointed

Best for: a first train set for a young child, especially one who already loves the Hayabusa. Skip it if: the listing is priced well above other Plarail starter sets. Any current Plarail starter set does the same job, and the track is shared across the range.

Plarail "Kimi ga Unten!" E5 Hayabusa: a child-sized driver's controller

Kimi ga unten (きみが運転) means "you're the driver". This set pairs an E5 Hayabusa with a handheld controller modelled on a mascon, the master controller a real train driver uses, so the child controls the speed instead of just switching the train on. That is a real step up in play value from a plain Plarail train, and it suits children who have reached the "I want to drive it" stage. The extra controller also brings extra things to manage. Takara Tomy's page for the set lists one AA battery for the train and two AAA batteries for the controller, sold separately, so there are two battery sizes to keep in stock. Before you buy, check the listing for exactly what is in the box. Plarail controller sets and train-only items look very similar, and if no track is included you will need it separately.

✔ The child controls the speed, so there is more to do than with a basic Plarail train · battery-powered, so there is no mains-voltage issue · runs on the same Plarail track system

✘ More batteries and more parts than a basic set · box contents (with or without track) vary between similar-looking listings, so read carefully · still a toy, with the same plastic look as all Plarail

Best for: a child who already has some Plarail track, or is getting a starter set too, and wants to be the driver. Skip it if: the child is at the younger end of the age range and mostly likes watching trains go round. A plain set is simpler.

KATO E235 Yamanote Line basic set, 4 cars (10-1468S): Tokyo's everyday train in 1:150

This is the train you have most likely ridden in Tokyo. KATO's model of the E235 series is a 1:150 N gauge basic set (基本セット) of four cars, a fraction of the real train; KATO's range uses add-on sets (増結セット) to lengthen a train. For many owners, four cars are enough on a small layout or display shelf. The weak point is the "what else do I need" problem. A basic set is the train only. KATO sells track and a power pack separately, or together in its starter set (10-001), so a first-time buyer has to buy those as well. KATO also sells other E235 products, such as its "Tabi suru N gauge" E235 set, so product number 10-1468S is one specific item. If you want to match a particular look or extend an existing train, check the product number rather than relying on the name "E235".

✔ A scale model of Tokyo's best-known commuter train · 1:150 scale, as is standard for Japanese N gauge models of regular-gauge trains · can be extended with add-on sets · the model itself has no country restriction; only the power pack does

✘ Train only, so track and a power pack for your country's mains supply are extra · four cars is a short train compared with the real one · KATO sells more than one E235 product, so check numbers before buying add-ons

Best for: adults and older teens who rode the Yamanote Line and want an accurate souvenir, and anyone who already owns N gauge track. Skip it if: the train is for a small child. It is a detailed model with small parts, not a toy to be pushed round the floor.

KATO N700S Nozomi basic set, 4 cars (10-1697S): the current Tokaido flagship

The N700S is the train that now runs most of the fastest services between Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka. KATO's basic set gives you four cars at 1:160, the scale used for Japanese Shinkansen models because the real line is standard gauge. The real train is much longer; KATO also sells a separate 16-car N700S set (10-1742), so a full-length model means a bigger purchase and the space to run it. Even four cars of a Shinkansen nose-and-tail set look long on a small oval. As with the E235, the set contains the train only. A less obvious point: the listing's own selling point is a nose shape that differs from the older N700A, a difference many non-enthusiasts will not notice. If the recipient mainly wants "a bullet train", the green-and-white E5 may be the more recognisable choice.

✔ Models the current Tokaido and Sanyo Shinkansen flagship · 1:160, the standard scale for Japanese Shinkansen N gauge · expandable toward a full-length train · the model itself has no country restriction; only the power pack does

✘ Train only: track and a power pack are extra · a full-length Shinkansen is very long and expensive to complete · differences from the older N700A are subtle

Best for: someone who travelled Tokyo to Kyoto or Osaka and wants the train they rode, built to scale. Skip it if: you have no room for a long train or no plans for a layout. A short commuter set like the E235 fits small spaces better.

Kato USA M1 Unitrack basic oval with power pack: the piece that makes the KATO trains run

This is not a train. It is the track and power you need before either KATO set above can move. Kato USA, KATO's North American arm, sells this M1 starter: an oval of Unitrack, KATO's snap-together track, with a rerailing section and a Kato power pack. It is sold for the North American market, but the listing does not state the power pack's input voltage, so check it against your outlet before you plug it in. Readers in the UK, Europe or Australia should look for a controller sold for their own 230 V or 240 V supply instead. An oval is also only a starting point. Expect to want sidings and switches (turnouts) eventually. A long Shinkansen set will also look cramped on a small oval.

✔ Track and a power pack in one box from KATO's North American arm · Unitrack snaps together, so no layout board is needed to start · the same track system as KATO's Japanese range

✘ Sold for North America; not the right power pack for 230 V countries · a plain oval soon feels limited · contains no train, so it has to be bought alongside one

Best for: US buyers starting N gauge with one of the KATO sets above. Skip it if: you live outside North America. Buy an N scale controller sold for your mains supply and Unitrack separately.

What about TOMIX? TOMIX, made by Tomytec in the Takara Tomy group, is Japan's other major N gauge brand, with its own track system. The verdict: one pick per person

  • A young child who loves the Shinkansen: a Plarail E5 Hayabusa starter set. The entry set if you can still find it at a fair price, otherwise any current Plarail starter set.
  • A child who wants to drive the train: Plarail "Kimi ga Unten!" E5, checking whether track is included.
  • An adult who rode around Tokyo: KATO E235 Yamanote Line basic set. It is short, recognisable and fits a shelf.
  • An adult who rode Tokyo to Kyoto or Osaka: KATO N700S Nozomi basic set, if you have space for a long train.

Common questions

Q. Can Plarail trains run on N gauge track, or KATO trains on Plarail?
A. No. Plarail is a battery-powered toy system with its own plastic track. N gauge models run on 9 mm metal-railed track and take power from the rails through a power pack. The two systems are not compatible in either direction.

Q. Can I use a Japanese power pack outside Japan?
A. Don't plug one in directly. Japanese domestic power packs are designed for Japan's 100 V household supply. The trains themselves never plug into the wall, so use a controller sold for your own mains voltage, such as the power pack in Kato USA's North American set, or an N scale controller from a local shop.

Q. Is N gauge 1:150 or 1:160?
A. Both run on the same 9 mm track. Japanese N gauge models of ordinary narrow-gauge trains, such as the Yamanote Line E235, are 1:150. Japanese Shinkansen models, and most N scale models from outside Japan, are 1:160.

Q. What age is Plarail for, and when should a child move to N gauge?
A. Takara Tomy labels Plarail for ages 3 and up. N gauge models are detailed hobby products with small parts and are better suited to older children who can handle them carefully, or to adults. There is no fixed age to switch. It depends on whether the child wants to build and drive a layout or just play with trains.
